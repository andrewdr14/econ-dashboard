In September 2022, the British government announced a package of tax cuts without saying how it would pay for them or publishing an independent forecast. Within days, the interest rate on 30-year government bonds had jumped by more than a percentage point, the pound had touched a record low against the dollar, pension funds were scrambling for cash and the Bank of England had to step in to stop a market meltdown. Yet Japan, whose government owes far more relative to the size of its economy than Britain's does, has borrowed at rock-bottom rates for decades. Clearly the size of a country's debt alone does not tell you when it becomes a problem. To understand why, you need a little vocabulary, one simple equation, and an appreciation of something harder to measure: credibility.

## Deficit versus debt

These two words are often used interchangeably, but they mean different things.

- The **deficit** is a *flow*: the gap, in a given year, between what the government spends and what it raises in taxes and other revenue. It is like overspending on your credit card this month.
- The **debt** is a *stock*: the total amount the government owes, built up from all past deficits (minus any surpluses). It is like your total credit card balance.

Each year's deficit adds to the debt. A country can have a falling deficit and still a rising debt, as long as the deficit is above zero.

Economists also distinguish the **primary deficit**, which excludes interest payments, from the overall deficit. The primary balance shows whether the government is covering its day-to-day costs from taxes before the cost of past borrowing is counted.

Both are usually expressed as a share of **gross domestic product (GDP)**, the size of the economy. That is not a quirk. A £100 billion debt means something very different for an economy of £500 billion than for one of £3 trillion. GDP is a rough guide to the tax base a government can draw on.

| United States | over 120% (2024, gross) |
| France | about 113% (2024) |

| Germany | about 63% (2024) |

Figures vary by measure (gross or net, which parts of government), so treat them as broad orders of magnitude.

## The arithmetic of debt: r minus g

Here is the most useful equation in public finance, in words rather than symbols. The change in the debt-to-GDP ratio each year depends on three things:

1. The **interest rate** the government pays on its debt, usually written *r*.
2. The **growth rate** of the economy, usually written *g*. (Both are measured in the same way, either both in cash terms or both after inflation.)
3. The **primary balance**: whether the government is running a deficit or surplus before interest.

If *r* is less than *g*, the economy grows faster than the debt accumulates interest. Even with a small primary deficit, the debt ratio can be stable or fall. If *r* is greater than *g*, the debt ratio rises unless the government runs a primary surplus big enough to offset the gap.

A worked example. Suppose debt is 100% of GDP, the government runs a balanced primary budget, and *r* is 4% while *g* is also 4%. The debt grows by 4% a year from interest, but so does GDP, so the ratio stays at 100%. Now raise *r* to 5% and keep *g* at 4%. The debt ratio creeps up by roughly one percentage point a year. To hold it steady, the government would need a primary surplus of about 1% of GDP, which in the UK in 2024 would have meant tax rises or spending cuts of around £28 billion a year.

This is why the era of very low interest rates, roughly 2009 to 2021, made high debt look cheap. In a widely discussed 2019 lecture, Olivier Blanchard argued that with *r* below *g* the fiscal cost of debt might be lower than commonly thought. When rates rose sharply in 2022 and 2023, that comfort faded, and debt interest became one of the fastest-growing items of public spending.

> When interest rates are below growth, debt can shrink relative to the economy almost by itself. When they rise above it, a country must run surpluses just to stand still.

## Why governments borrow at all

Debt is not inherently bad:

- **Smoothing shocks.** In a recession, tax revenues fall and spending on benefits rises automatically. Borrowing lets governments cushion the blow instead of making cuts that would deepen the downturn.
- **Emergencies.** Wars and pandemics are classic cases. UK government debt was more than 250% of GDP after the Second World War, and was then steadily reduced over the following decades through growth, inflation and modest primary surpluses.
- **Investment.** Borrowing to build roads, railways or research labs can make sense if the projects raise future output, much as a household might take a mortgage.

The trouble comes when borrowing is persistent, not tied to investment, and when lenders start to doubt that it will be repaid on the terms they expected.

## Who holds the debt matters

Government debt is mostly sold in the form of bonds: in the UK, **gilts**; in the US, Treasuries. Who buys them makes a difference.

- **Domestic savers and institutions**, such as pension funds and insurers, tend to be stable holders. Japan's debt is overwhelmingly held at home, a large part of it by the Bank of Japan itself.
- **Foreign investors** can be more fickle. They may sell quickly if they lose confidence, and their selling can weaken the currency at the same time.
- **The central bank**, through quantitative easing, became a major holder of government debt in many countries after 2008. That can lower borrowing costs but also blurs the line between government and central bank finances.

The **maturity** of debt also matters. UK debt has an unusually long average maturity, around 14 years in recent years, which means rising interest rates feed through slowly. However, a sizeable share is index-linked to inflation, which made UK debt costs jump in 2022.

## Your own currency, or someone else's?

Perhaps the single biggest distinction is whether a country borrows in a currency it controls.

The UK, US and Japan borrow in pounds, dollars and yen, which their own central banks issue. Such governments cannot be forced into default in the way a household can, because, in the last resort, money can be created to pay bondholders. That does not make debt free. Excessive money creation can cause inflation and a falling currency, which impose real costs and amount to a kind of default by stealth. But it does remove one specific danger: running out of the currency you owe.

Countries in the euro area borrow in a currency none of them individually controls. Italy cannot ask the Banca d'Italia to print euros; that power sits with the European Central Bank. Neither can emerging economies that borrow in dollars. These countries face a genuine risk of a **self-fulfilling crisis**: if investors fear default, they demand higher interest rates, which make default more likely, which raises rates further.

## Three episodes

### The UK gilt crisis of 2022

On 23 September 2022, Chancellor Kwasi Kwarteng's "mini-budget" announced around £45 billion a year of unfunded tax cuts, alongside an expensive energy support scheme, at a time when inflation was already near 10% and the Bank of England was raising rates. Markets reacted badly. Gilt yields rose at one of the fastest rates on record.

The damage was magnified by a corner of the pensions industry. Many defined-benefit pension schemes used **liability-driven investment** (LDI) strategies, which relied on borrowing against gilts. As gilt prices fell, they faced demands for more collateral and had to sell gilts to raise cash, pushing prices down further. On 28 September the Bank of England announced temporary purchases of long-dated gilts to break the spiral. Most of the measures were reversed within weeks, Mr Kwarteng was replaced, and Liz Truss resigned as prime minister after about seven weeks in office.

The lesson most economists drew was not that UK debt was suddenly unsustainable but that credibility matters: markets punished large unfunded tax cuts, the sidelining of the independent Office for Budget Responsibility and a clash with the central bank's fight against inflation. The LDI plumbing made the reaction far worse than the fiscal news alone would have.

### Japan: the great puzzle

Japan's gross government debt has exceeded 200% of GDP since around 2010, the highest in the developed world. For most of that time it borrowed at interest rates close to zero.

Several factors explain the calm. The debt is almost entirely in yen and held at home, much of it funded by Japanese households' heavy saving via banks and pension funds. The Bank of Japan held around half of all government bonds by the early 2020s. And for years Japan battled deflation, so there was little pressure to raise rates.

As inflation returned after 2022 and the Bank of Japan began raising rates and stepping back from bond buying, yields rose. How Japan copes with *r* no longer near zero is one of the big fiscal questions of the coming decade.

### Greece: the warning

In late 2009, the new Greek government revealed that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported: more than 12% of GDP, later revised higher still. Greek debt was then about 125% of GDP, much lower than Japan's today. But Greece used the euro, much of its debt was held abroad, and its statistics had lost credibility.

Greek bond yields soared. In 2010 Greece received the first of three bailouts from euro area governments and the International Monetary Fund, conditional on deep austerity, and in 2012 private bondholders took large losses in the biggest sovereign debt restructuring in history. Greek GDP fell by roughly a quarter from its 2008 peak, and unemployment rose above 25%. The episode shows how quickly debt can become a crisis when a country cannot print its own currency and loses market trust.

## Fiscal rules

To reassure lenders and voters, many governments bind themselves with **fiscal rules**. The EU's rules have long set reference values of a 3% of GDP deficit and 60% of GDP debt, dating back to the Maastricht Treaty of 1992; they were reformed in 2024 to focus more on country-specific spending paths. The UK has had a long series of rules, rewritten frequently by successive chancellors. As of mid-2026 they centre on balancing day-to-day spending with revenue and getting a broad measure of debt falling as a share of GDP within a few years, with borrowing for investment treated more generously. Germany has a constitutional "debt brake", loosened in 2025 to allow more spending on defence and infrastructure.

Critics say rules are arbitrary, invite accounting tricks and can force cuts at the wrong moment. Supporters say they anchor expectations and keep borrowing costs down. Both may be right.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Government debt is just like household debt."** Partly true, but governments can last indefinitely, tax future generations and, if they borrow in their own currency, create money. That changes the arithmetic.
- **"There is a magic threshold."** A famous 2010 study by Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff suggested growth slowed sharply above 90% of debt to GDP; later work found errors in the data and weaker evidence for any sharp cliff. There is no universal tipping point.
- **"Debt doesn't matter at all."** Higher debt means higher interest bills, less room to respond to the next crisis and greater exposure to shifts in market sentiment.
- **"We owe it to ourselves."** Even domestically held debt redistributes from taxpayers to bondholders, and much debt is held abroad.

## Key takeaways

- The deficit is the annual gap between spending and revenue; the debt is the accumulated total. Both are best judged relative to GDP.
- The gap between the interest rate on debt and the economy's growth rate (*r* minus *g*) largely determines whether a debt ratio rises or falls.
- Borrowing in your own currency, with patient domestic holders, gives far more room than borrowing in a currency you don't control, as Japan and Greece show.
- Credibility is crucial: the UK's 2022 gilt crisis showed markets can react sharply to policies that look unfunded, even when debt is moderate by international standards.
- There is no single safe level of debt, which is why fiscal rules are both useful and contested.
