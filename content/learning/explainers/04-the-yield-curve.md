Few charts in finance have a reputation quite like it. For decades, one simple line on a graph, plotting what the US government pays to borrow over different lengths of time, turned upside down before almost every American recession. Economists, investors and journalists came to treat it as something close to an oracle. When it flipped in 2022, headlines warned that a downturn was on its way.

Then something strange happened. The curve stayed inverted for longer than ever before, and the US economy kept growing through 2023 and 2024. Was the oracle broken, or merely early? To answer that, we need to understand what the yield curve is, why it usually slopes upwards, and what it means when it doesn't.

## First, what is a bond yield?

When a government needs to borrow, it sells **bonds**: promises to pay the holder a fixed amount of interest each year (the **coupon**) and return the original sum (the **principal**) on a set date, known as **maturity**. UK government bonds are called **gilts**; US ones are **Treasuries**; Germany's are **Bunds**, which serve as the euro area's benchmark.

Once issued, bonds trade in the market, and their prices move. The **yield** is the annual return you would get if you bought the bond at today's price and held it to maturity. Prices and yields move in opposite directions. Suppose a bond pays £5 a year on £100. If its price falls to £90, a buyer still gets £5 a year, plus a £10 gain at maturity, so the yield rises. When people say "gilt yields jumped", they mean bond prices fell.

## The yield curve

Governments borrow over many different horizons, from a few months to 30 years or more. The **yield curve** plots the yields on these bonds against their maturity: three months on the left, perhaps 30 years on the right.

Most of the time, the curve slopes **upwards**. Borrowing for ten years costs more than borrowing for two. When the curve slopes **downwards**, so that short-term yields exceed long-term yields, it is said to be **inverted**.

Analysts usually summarise the curve with a single **spread**. The two most watched in the US are:

- **10-year minus 2-year** (the "2s10s"), popular with markets.
- **10-year minus 3-month**, favoured by many researchers, including those at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, which publishes a recession-probability model based on it.

## Why the curve normally slopes upwards

There are two main ingredients in a long-term yield.

### Expectations of future short-term rates

Imagine you want to invest for two years. You could buy a two-year bond, or buy a one-year bond and then reinvest in another one-year bond next year. If investors are rational, those strategies should offer roughly similar expected returns. That means the two-year yield reflects, approximately, an average of today's one-year rate and the one-year rate expected next year.

Extend that logic, and a ten-year yield reflects the average of short-term interest rates that markets expect over the next decade. This is called the **expectations hypothesis**. If markets expect the central bank to raise rates, long yields will be above short ones. If they expect cuts, long yields will be lower.

### The term premium

The second ingredient is compensation for risk. Locking money away for ten years exposes you to the chance that inflation or interest rates rise unexpectedly, eroding the value of your bond. Investors usually demand extra yield for bearing that risk. This is the **term premium**.

Because the term premium is usually positive and rate expectations are usually fairly balanced, the curve normally slopes upwards. The term premium cannot be observed directly, though; it has to be estimated with models, and estimates differ. In the decade after 2008, with central banks buying vast quantities of bonds through quantitative easing, many estimates suggested the term premium had fallen close to zero or even below it.

## What an inversion is telling you

If the curve inverts, the most natural reading is that markets expect short-term interest rates to fall significantly in future. Why would they expect that? Usually because they think the central bank will have to cut rates in response to an economic slowdown.

Inversions typically happen late in a cycle of rate rises. The central bank pushes short-term rates up to cool an overheating economy. Long-term yields rise by less, because investors expect the tightening to slow the economy and eventually force cuts. At some point short rates overtake long ones.

There is also a mechanical story. Banks broadly borrow short (taking deposits) and lend long (making loans and mortgages). An inverted curve squeezes the margin between the two, which can make banks less willing to lend, and that in itself can slow the economy.

## The track record

The idea that the curve predicts recessions has been studied since the 1980s. The economist Campbell Harvey linked it to economic growth in his doctoral research in 1986, and subsequent work, including by Arturo Estrella and Frederic Mishkin at the New York Fed in the 1990s, found it one of the most reliable single predictors of US recessions.

The record is genuinely striking. Since the late 1960s, each US recession has been preceded by an inversion of the 10-year minus 3-month spread, with very few false alarms. The lead time is long and variable, typically somewhere between six months and two years. In 2019 the curve briefly inverted, and a recession arrived in 2020, though that one was caused by a pandemic, which no bond market could have predicted. Many count it as a lucky hit rather than a real signal.

Outside the US the record is weaker. UK and German curves have inverted at various points, but their predictive power is generally found to be less reliable, partly because their bond markets are influenced heavily by global forces and by US yields.

## Why 2022-24 was unusual

In July 2022, the US 2s10s spread inverted. By late 2022 the 10-year minus 3-month spread had followed. Both stayed inverted until late 2024, making it the longest stretch of inversion on record. Gilt and Bund curves also inverted during this period.

Yet the US did not fall into recession in 2023 or 2024. Growth was robust and unemployment stayed low. Several explanations have been put forward, and they are not mutually exclusive.

1. **The inflation shock was different.** Inversions usually reflect expectations that a slowdown will force rate cuts. This time, markets could reasonably expect rates to fall simply because inflation would come down from a temporary spike, without the economy needing to crash. Expected cuts did not have to mean an expected recession.
2. **The term premium was unusually low.** Years of QE had pushed down long-term yields relative to where they would otherwise be. That made inversion easier to achieve without a strong recession signal.
3. **Households and firms were insulated.** Many had locked in very low fixed rates on mortgages and corporate debt in 2020-21, so higher short-term rates took longer to bite. In the US, 30-year fixed mortgages meant many homeowners barely noticed.
4. **Fiscal policy and savings.** Large government deficits and savings built up during the pandemic supported spending.

There was, however, some stress. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank collapsed, in part because it had invested heavily in long-term bonds whose value fell as rates rose. The episode was a reminder that rapid rate rises and an inverted curve do put strain on banks.

## Why it matters beyond the trading floor

The yield curve is not just a curiosity for bond traders. Long-term government yields act as a benchmark for a great deal of other borrowing. In the UK, the rates on two-year and five-year fixed mortgages track gilt yields and related market rates for those horizons far more closely than they track Bank Rate itself. In the US, 30-year mortgage rates move broadly with the 10-year Treasury yield.

So the shape of the curve affects household budgets directly. The UK saw this vividly in September 2022, when the government's "mini-budget" of unfunded tax cuts triggered a sharp sell-off in gilts. Yields jumped, lenders pulled hundreds of mortgage products, and new fixed-rate deals became much more expensive within days. The Bank of England stepped in to buy long-dated gilts to restore order, after the turmoil threatened pension funds using certain hedging strategies. The episode showed that bond markets can move independently of the central bank, and that the yield curve reflects judgements about government finances as well as about the economy.

## Caveats worth remembering

- **Correlation is not a law.** The curve has a strong record, but it rests on a fairly small number of recessions. Ten or so events is not much for statistical certainty.
- **The signal can be self-defeating.** If everyone believes inversion means recession, central banks and governments may respond early, preventing the very downturn predicted.
- **Timing is vague.** A warning that may come true at any point between six months and two years is of limited use to anyone trying to time markets.
- **The re-steepening matters too.** Historically, recessions have often started not while the curve was inverted but shortly after it turned positive again, as the central bank began cutting. Some analysts therefore argue that the end of an inversion is the more dangerous moment.
- **Different spreads give different signals.** The 2s10s and 10-year minus 3-month spreads do not always invert at the same time.

## Common misconceptions

- **"An inverted curve causes recessions."** It mainly reflects market expectations. It can contribute a little through bank lending, but it is chiefly a thermometer, not the fever.
- **"The yield curve is a reliable timing tool for investors."** The lead times are too long and variable for that. This is not investment advice, and the curve should not be used as a trading signal on its own.
- **"Because 2022-24 didn't produce a recession, the indicator is now useless."** It may have been distorted by unusual conditions. Economists continue to debate how much weight it deserves.

## Key takeaways

- A bond's yield is the return from buying it at today's price; prices and yields move in opposite directions.
- The yield curve normally slopes upwards because of expected future rates and the term premium for locking money away.
- An inversion, with short yields above long ones, usually signals that markets expect rate cuts, often because of a slowdown.
- Inversions preceded every US recession since the late 1960s, but the record rests on few cases and the lead times vary widely.
- The record-long inversion of 2022-24 was not followed by recession in 2023 or 2024, probably because of an unusual mix of an inflation shock, a low term premium and insulated borrowers.
