In March 2009, with the world economy in free fall, the Bank of England did something it had never done before. It had already cut Bank Rate to 0.5%, the lowest in its then 315-year history, and that did not seem enough. So it announced it would create £75 billion of new money and use it to buy assets, mostly government bonds. Over the next 12 years that programme would grow to £895 billion. The US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan did something similar on an even larger scale. The policy was called quantitative easing, or QE, and it was described in newspapers as "printing money". That phrase is understandable but misleading. To understand what QE really is, and why it remains controversial, it helps to start with the problem it was designed to solve.

## The problem: the zero lower bound

Central banks normally steer the economy by setting a short-term interest rate. When the economy weakens, they cut it. Cheaper borrowing encourages households and firms to spend and invest, and lower returns on savings nudge people to spend rather than hoard.

But there is a limit. Interest rates cannot easily go much below zero. If banks charged savers substantially negative rates, people could simply withdraw cash, which pays zero. This floor is known as the **zero lower bound**, or more accurately the effective lower bound, since a few central banks, including the ECB, the Swiss National Bank and the Bank of Japan, did push rates slightly below zero in the 2010s.

By early 2009, rates in the US and UK were close to zero, and the economy still needed more help. Central banks needed another tool. Japan had already experimented with QE between 2001 and 2006, as it battled deflation, which gave others a template of sorts.

## How QE works, step by step

The mechanics are simpler than they sound.

1. The central bank decides to buy assets, usually government bonds, from investors such as pension funds and insurance companies.
2. It pays for them by crediting the accounts of the sellers' banks with new **central bank reserves**, electronic money that banks hold at the central bank.
3. The seller's bank in turn credits the seller's account with a matching deposit.

No banknotes are printed. What is created is reserves, a kind of money only banks can hold, plus an equal rise in bank deposits. The central bank's balance sheet expands: it now owns bonds on one side and owes reserves on the other.

In the UK, the purchases were made by a subsidiary, the Asset Purchase Facility, which was financed by a loan from the Bank and indemnified by the Treasury. That indemnity matters, as we will see.

> QE swaps one kind of asset that the private sector holds, long-term bonds, for another, central bank reserves. The magic, if there is any, lies in how that swap changes prices and behaviour.

The scale became vast. The Fed's balance sheet grew from under $1 trillion before 2008 to about $9 trillion by 2022. The ECB began large-scale purchases of government bonds in 2015 and expanded them massively during the pandemic. The Bank of Japan ended up owning around half of all Japanese government bonds.

## The channels: why might QE work?

Economists identify several routes through which QE could affect the economy.

### Portfolio rebalancing

When the central bank buys large amounts of government bonds, it pushes up their prices and lowers their **yields** (the return they offer, which moves inversely to price). Investors who sold bonds now hold cash they want to reinvest. They buy other assets, such as corporate bonds and shares, pushing up their prices too. Lower long-term interest rates make mortgages and business borrowing cheaper, and higher asset prices make people feel wealthier. This is generally thought to be the main channel.

### Signalling

QE can also act as a promise. By buying bonds on a large scale, a central bank shows it is serious about supporting the economy and that it is unlikely to raise short-term rates soon. That expectation itself lowers longer-term rates.

### Market functioning

In a panic, markets can seize up as everyone tries to sell at once. A central bank standing ready to buy can restore order. This was a key motive for the huge purchases in March 2020, when even the US Treasury market, normally the deepest and most liquid in the world, became dysfunctional.

### The exchange rate

Lower yields can make a currency less attractive to foreign investors, weakening it and giving exporters a boost.

### Bank lending

More reserves in the banking system might, in principle, encourage banks to lend more. In practice, this channel was thought weak after 2008, when banks were trying to repair their balance sheets rather than expand them. The textbook "money multiplier", in which each pound of reserves becomes many pounds of loans, did not show up.

## What did it achieve?

Measuring the effects of QE is hard, because we cannot see what would have happened without it. Economists have used two broad approaches: studying how markets reacted on the days QE was announced, and building models of the wider economy.

The event studies suggest QE had substantial effects on bond yields, especially early on. Bank of England research estimated that the first £200 billion of purchases, completed by early 2010, lowered gilt yields by about one percentage point. Studies of the Fed's first programmes found similar-sized effects on US Treasury yields.

Translating that into growth and inflation is more uncertain. Bank of England estimates suggested the first round raised GDP by around 1.5% to 2% and inflation by between 0.75 and 1.5 percentage points at their peak. Fed and ECB research found broadly comparable effects. Many economists concluded that QE helped prevent the financial crisis from turning into a deeper depression and helped ward off deflation.

There is reasonable evidence, too, that the impact faded over time. Later rounds of QE, announced when markets were calm and rates were already low, appeared to move yields less. Purchases made in a crisis, to calm frantic markets, seem to have had the most bang for the buck.

Sceptics question how much of the effect was QE itself rather than the accompanying signals about interest rates, and some argue that the economic recovery of the 2010s was disappointingly slow despite QE, suggesting its power was limited.

## Who benefits? Asset prices and inequality

QE works partly by pushing up the prices of assets: bonds, shares and, indirectly, houses. Since those assets are owned disproportionately by older and wealthier people, critics argue QE widened wealth inequality.

The Bank of England addressed this in a 2012 report on the distributional effects of its asset purchases. It acknowledged that holders of financial assets gained, and that the richest 5% of households held around 40% of those assets. Savers relying on interest income were hurt by low rates.

Central banks make a counter-argument. Without QE, they say, the recession would have been deeper and unemployment higher, which would have hit poorer households hardest, through job losses and lower wages. A fair comparison is not with a world of normal interest rates but with a world of deeper slump. On this view, QE's effect on *income* inequality may have been small, even if its effect on *wealth* inequality was larger.

The debate also touched on housing. Low long-term rates helped push up house prices in many countries, which benefited existing owners and made it harder for younger people to get on the ladder, though low interest rates more generally, not just QE, played a part.

## Did QE cause the inflation of 2021-23?

This is contested. Some economists, especially those focused on the money supply, argue that the pandemic-era burst of QE, combined with large government deficits, flooded economies with money and planted the seeds of inflation. Broad money growth in the US and UK did spike sharply in 2020 and 2021.

Most central banks argue the main causes lay elsewhere: the energy shock after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, supply chain disruption and very strong demand as economies reopened. A middle view is that QE in 2020 and 2021, alongside low rates and fiscal support, added to demand at a time when supply was constrained, and that central banks were too slow to stop buying and start raising rates. The Bank of England, for instance, continued buying bonds until December 2021, when UK inflation was already above 5%.

## Quantitative tightening and central bank losses

From 2022, as inflation surged, central banks began to reverse course. **Quantitative tightening (QT)** means shrinking the central bank's balance sheet, either by not reinvesting the money when bonds mature or, more unusually, by actively selling bonds back to the market. The Bank of England began active sales in November 2022, making it an outlier among major central banks. The Fed relied on letting bonds mature without replacing them, and in late 2025 it brought its balance sheet reduction to an end.

QT raised an awkward issue: losses. When central banks bought bonds in the 2010s, interest rates were low, and bonds were expensive. The reserves created to pay for them earn the central bank's policy rate. While rates were near zero, the arrangement was profitable, and the Bank of England's asset purchase facility transferred around £120 billion of surplus to the Treasury.

Once interest rates rose sharply in 2022 and 2023, the position flipped. The central bank was now paying 4% or 5% on reserves while earning much less on its old, low-coupon bonds. Selling bonds that had fallen in price locked in further losses. Because of the Treasury indemnity, in the UK these losses are ultimately borne by taxpayers. The Office for Budget Responsibility has estimated that the lifetime net cost of QE to the public finances could be of the order of £100 billion, though the figure depends heavily on future interest rates.

Some economists, and some politicians, have proposed changing how central banks pay interest on reserves, for example by not paying interest on part of them, to reduce these costs. Others warn that doing so would amount to a tax on banks and could weaken the central bank's control over interest rates.

There is also an argument about framing. The losses arise because interest rates are higher, which in turn means the government's own borrowing is costlier. Looking at QE in isolation, critics say, can be misleading; the relevant question is whether the policy improved the economy overall during the years it was in place.

## Common misconceptions

- **"QE is printing money to hand out."** No banknotes are printed, and the money is exchanged for assets rather than given away. That makes it different from directly funding government spending, at least in intention.
- **"QE is the same as financing the government."** Central banks bought bonds in the secondary market and insisted the purchases were to meet their inflation targets. Critics argue the line became blurred, especially in 2020, when purchases roughly matched new government borrowing.
- **"QE must cause hyperinflation."** After 2009, many predicted runaway inflation. It did not arrive for more than a decade, and when it did, it had several causes.

## Key takeaways

- QE was a response to the zero lower bound: when short-term rates could fall no further, central banks bought long-term bonds to push down longer-term interest rates.
- It works by creating central bank reserves to buy assets, mainly through portfolio rebalancing, signalling and restoring calm in panicking markets.
- Evidence suggests it lowered bond yields and supported growth and inflation, with the biggest effects in crises and smaller ones later.
- By lifting asset prices, QE benefited wealthier households, though central banks argue the alternative of a deeper slump would have been worse for the poor.
- Unwinding QE, alongside higher interest rates, has turned past profits into losses that, in the UK, fall on the public finances.
