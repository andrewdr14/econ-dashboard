In 1933, roughly a quarter of the American workforce was unemployed. In Britain, whole towns built on coal, steel and shipbuilding had been idle for years. Factories stood empty while people who wanted to work and had nothing to buy sat at home. Something had gone badly wrong, and the prevailing economics of the day had little to say about how to fix it.

Into this gap stepped John Maynard Keynes, a Cambridge economist, civil servant, investor and member of the Bloomsbury set. His book *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, published in 1936, argued that economies could get stuck in slumps for long periods, and that governments could, and should, do something about it. It became one of the most influential books of the twentieth century. Nearly a hundred years on, its ideas still shape what happens whenever recession threatens.

## The world Keynes was arguing against

Before Keynes, the dominant view, which he labelled "classical", held that market economies tend naturally towards full employment. If workers were unemployed, wages would fall until employers found it worthwhile to hire them. If people saved more, interest rates would fall, encouraging businesses to borrow and invest the savings. Supply, in a famous formulation associated with the French economist Jean-Baptiste Say, creates its own demand: producing goods generates the income to buy them.

On this view, slumps were temporary, and the best government response was usually to balance the budget and let prices adjust. Keynes's response to those who said things would sort themselves out in the long run is one of the most quoted lines in economics. Writing in 1923, he said: "In the long run we are all dead."

## Aggregate demand

The heart of Keynes's argument was the idea of **aggregate demand**: total spending in the economy. It has four parts: spending by households (consumption), by businesses on new equipment and buildings (investment), by government, and by foreigners on exports, minus spending on imports.

Keynes argued that output and employment are determined, in the short run, by how much people want to spend. If spending falls, firms sell less, so they produce less and lay off workers. Those workers then have less income, so they spend less, and the downturn feeds on itself.

Crucially, he argued that falling wages and prices would not necessarily fix this. Lower wages cut workers' incomes and therefore their spending. Falling prices increase the real burden of debts, pushing borrowers to cut back further. An economy could settle at an equilibrium with persistently high unemployment, with no automatic force to drag it out.

Investment was especially volatile, Keynes believed, because it depends on business confidence about an uncertain future. He described the urge to act, rather than calculate, as **"animal spirits"**. When those spirits collapse, investment can collapse with them.

## The multiplier

If private spending falls short, Keynes argued, government spending can fill the gap. And it may have an outsized effect, through what is called the **multiplier**, an idea developed by his Cambridge colleague Richard Kahn in 1931.

Suppose the government spends £100 million building a bridge. That money becomes wages and profits for builders and suppliers. They spend some of it, say 80%, in shops and restaurants, and save the rest. That £80 million becomes someone else's income, of which 80% is again spent, and so on. Add up all the rounds and the total boost to spending is far bigger than the original £100 million. In this simple example, it is five times as large.

Real-world multipliers are much smaller than textbook ones, because money leaks out of the cycle through taxes, saving and imports. Estimates vary widely, typically somewhere between about 0.5 and 2, and are fiercely debated. An important finding is that multipliers seem to be larger in deep recessions, when there are lots of idle resources and interest rates are near zero, than in booms. In 2013, IMF economists Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh published research suggesting that forecasters had underestimated multipliers during the euro area crisis, meaning austerity had damaged growth more than expected.

## The paradox of thrift

One of Keynes's most counterintuitive ideas is the **paradox of thrift**. For an individual family, saving more in hard times is sensible. But if every family does so at once, total spending falls, incomes fall, and the economy may end up with less output and, potentially, no more saving than before. What is wise for each person can be harmful for all together.

This insight underpins an argument still heard today: when households and businesses are cutting back, a government that also cuts back may deepen the slump. Critics reply that it is easily overstated, especially when an economy is not in a deep recession.

## The liquidity trap

Classical economists expected lower interest rates to revive investment. Keynes recognised a limit. When interest rates are already very low, people may simply hoard money rather than buy bonds, and further monetary stimulus achieves little. This became known as the **liquidity trap**.

For decades it seemed a theoretical curiosity. Then Japan in the 1990s, and much of the developed world after 2008, found itself with interest rates near zero and economies still weak. Keynes's idea suddenly looked prescient, and it strengthened the argument that fiscal policy has a vital role when monetary policy runs out of room.

## The post-war consensus

The Second World War was, among other things, a vast Keynesian experiment: huge government spending eliminated unemployment almost overnight. Afterwards, Keynesian ideas became mainstream policy. The UK government's 1944 White Paper on Employment Policy accepted the maintenance of "a high and stable level of employment" as a responsibility of government. In the US, the Employment Act of 1946 did something similar.

Keynes himself was a leading negotiator at the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, which created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank and established the post-war system of fixed exchange rates. He died in 1946.

For roughly a quarter of a century, governments across the West tried to "fine-tune" demand, using taxes and spending to smooth out booms and busts. The era coincided with fast growth, low unemployment and rising living standards, sometimes called the "golden age" of capitalism. How much credit Keynesian policy deserves for that, as opposed to post-war reconstruction, catch-up growth and cheap energy, is still debated.

## The fall

The 1970s were brutal for Keynesianism. Economies suffered **stagflation**: high inflation and high unemployment at the same time. The simple Keynesian framework, which suggested a trade-off between the two, struggled to explain how both could rise together. Attempts to spend out of trouble seemed to produce more inflation rather than more jobs.

Monetarists led by Milton Friedman, and later economists of the "rational expectations" school such as Robert Lucas, argued that demand management was at best ineffective and at worst harmful. In Britain, Prime Minister James Callaghan told the 1976 Labour Party conference that the option of spending your way out of a recession no longer existed. By the 1980s, controlling inflation, rather than maintaining full employment, had become the main goal of economic policy, and monetary policy had become the main tool.

Keynesianism did not disappear, though. It was absorbed into a **New Keynesian** synthesis that accepted many monetarist points about expectations and inflation but kept the idea that prices and wages adjust slowly, so demand matters in the short run. Modern central bank models are built largely on this foundation.

## The revival: 2008 and 2020

The global financial crisis brought Keynes back into the spotlight. With interest rates at or near zero, governments around the world launched large stimulus packages. The US passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act in 2009, worth roughly $800 billion. China announced a stimulus of around 4 trillion yuan. The UK temporarily cut VAT.

The revival was short-lived in some places. From 2010, many European governments, including the UK, turned to **austerity**, arguing that high public debt posed the bigger danger. Whether austerity slowed the recovery remains one of the most contested questions in recent economic history.

The pandemic of 2020 produced an even bigger Keynesian response. Governments paid wages directly through schemes such as the UK's furlough scheme, sent cheques to households, and lent heavily to businesses. The US alone passed several packages worth trillions of dollars. Unemployment rose less than many feared, and recoveries were faster than after 2008. But the inflation of 2021-23 led critics, including some prominent Keynesian economists such as Larry Summers, to argue that the stimulus, especially in the US, had been too large and contributed to overheating.

## The critiques

Keynesian economics has always attracted serious objections.

- **Crowding out.** Government borrowing may push up interest rates and displace private investment, reducing the net effect of stimulus.
- **Ricardian equivalence.** The economist Robert Barro argued in 1974 that rational households, anticipating future taxes to repay government debt, might save any tax cut rather than spend it. The evidence suggests this holds only partially.
- **Timing.** By the time a stimulus package is designed, debated and implemented, the recession may be over, and the extra spending may add to inflation.
- **Political economy.** Governments find it easy to spend in bad times but hard to cut back in good times, so Keynesian policy may in practice ratchet up public debt.
- **The Lucas critique.** Robert Lucas argued in 1976 that relationships observed in past data may change when policy changes, because people adapt their behaviour.

Supporters respond that Keynes's core insights remain hard to dispute. Demand does fall in recessions, idle resources are wasteful, and in deep slumps with interest rates near zero, government spending can make a real difference. Most modern economists, including many who are sceptical of fine-tuning, accept a role for fiscal policy in severe downturns, as well as for "automatic stabilisers": tax receipts that fall and benefit payments that rise automatically when the economy weakens.

## Key takeaways

- Keynes argued, against the classical view, that economies can get stuck in prolonged slumps because of insufficient total spending.
- The multiplier means public spending can have an amplified effect, especially in deep recessions with interest rates near zero, though real multipliers are smaller and more uncertain than in textbooks.
- The paradox of thrift and the liquidity trap explain why private caution and cheap money may not be enough to end a slump.
- Keynesianism dominated post-war policy, faltered with 1970s stagflation, and was absorbed into New Keynesian economics.
- Its ideas returned in force in 2008 and 2020, while debates over austerity and post-pandemic inflation keep the arguments about it alive.
