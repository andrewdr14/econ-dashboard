A British worker today produces several times as much in an hour as her great-great-grandparents did in the 1890s. That is the single biggest reason why we live longer, eat better, travel further and work fewer hours than people did a century ago. It is not because we work harder; on the whole we work less. It is because each hour of work produces far more. Economists call this **productivity**, and in 1990 Paul Krugman summed up its importance in a line that has been quoted ever since: "Productivity isn't everything, but in the long run it is almost everything." This article explains what productivity is, where it comes from, why Britain's has stalled, why it is so hard to measure, and whether artificial intelligence might change the picture.

## What productivity means

At its simplest, productivity is **output per unit of input**. The most common measure is **labour productivity**: the value of goods and services produced, adjusted for inflation, divided by the number of hours worked. Some statistics use output per worker instead, but output per hour is usually better because it does not count a part-time worker as producing "less" simply for working fewer hours.

Why does this matter so much? Because over the long run, wages track productivity. A firm cannot pay workers more, year after year, than the value they produce. If output per hour doubles, the economy can afford to pay roughly twice as much per hour, or to give people more leisure, or some mix of the two. Short-term booms can raise incomes temporarily, by getting more people into work or by borrowing, but only rising productivity can raise living standards decade after decade.

The power of compounding makes small differences enormous. Using the "rule of 70", an economy growing at 2% a year doubles its output per hour in about 35 years. At 0.5% a year, doubling takes around 140 years. That is roughly the difference between parents expecting their children to be twice as well off as them, and needing to wait for their great-great-grandchildren.

> A difference of a percentage point or so in annual productivity growth sounds trivial. Over a working lifetime it is the difference between transformation and stagnation.

## Where productivity growth comes from

Economists usually break the sources of growth into three parts, a method known as **growth accounting**, developed by Robert Solow and others in the 1950s.

### More capital per worker

Give a worker better tools and she produces more. A builder with a digger moves more earth than one with a spade; an accountant with modern software processes more invoices than one with a ledger. Increasing the amount of machinery, equipment, buildings and software per hour worked is called **capital deepening**.

### Better-skilled workers

Education and training make workers more capable. Economists call this **human capital**. The spread of secondary and university education over the twentieth century contributed substantially to productivity growth in rich countries.

### Total factor productivity

Then there is everything else. Once you account for more capital and better skills, a large part of growth is left unexplained. Solow found in a famous 1957 paper that most of US growth in output per hour in the first half of the twentieth century could not be explained by more capital alone. This leftover became known as the **Solow residual**, or **total factor productivity (TFP)**.

TFP captures how efficiently capital and labour are combined. It includes new technologies and ideas, better management, more efficient organisation, and resources moving from less productive firms to more productive ones. Some economists jokingly call it "a measure of our ignorance", because it is calculated as whatever is left over rather than measured directly. But in the long run it is what matters most, because capital deepening runs into diminishing returns: giving a worker a second digger adds much less than the first.

## Ideas, diffusion and the role of firms

If TFP depends on ideas, where do ideas come from? Partly from research and development, in universities and in companies. But for most firms, the more important question is how quickly they adopt ideas that already exist.

Research by the OECD and others in the 2010s found a widening gap between the most productive "frontier" firms in each industry and the rest. The best firms kept improving; the long tail of laggards did not catch up. In the UK, studies have found an unusually long tail of firms with poor management practices and low productivity. Getting the average firm closer to the best can matter more for national productivity than pushing the frontier further.

Other ingredients matter too: competition, which pushes firms to improve or exit; infrastructure such as transport and digital networks; a financial system that channels money to good projects; and sensible planning rules that let cities grow where firms want to locate.

## The UK productivity puzzle

For several decades before the 2008 financial crisis, UK labour productivity grew at around 2% a year. Since then it has grown at less than half that pace, by most estimates somewhere around 0.5% a year in the decade after 2008. Had the pre-crisis trend continued, output per hour would be roughly 20% or more higher today than it is.

Other rich countries also slowed after 2008, including the United States, Germany and France, but the UK's slowdown was among the sharpest in the G7. On the ONS's international comparisons, UK output per hour has in recent years been below that of the US, France and Germany, by margins in the range of about 10% to 20% depending on the year and measure, though such comparisons are sensitive to methodology.

Economists have proposed many explanations. None is decisive alone.

1. **The financial sector.** Before 2008, UK productivity was flattered by a booming financial sector. Its contraction removed a big source of measured growth, and some of the pre-crisis gains may have been illusory.
2. **Weak investment.** Business investment in the UK has been low by international standards for decades, and was held back further by uncertainty after the 2016 Brexit referendum. Less investment means less capital deepening.
3. **"Zombie" firms.** Very low interest rates after 2008 may have kept unproductive firms alive, trapping workers and capital where they were least useful. The evidence on how much this mattered is mixed.
4. **Cheap and flexible labour.** With real wages falling after 2008 and plenty of workers available, firms may have chosen to hire people rather than invest in labour-saving machinery. That kept employment high, which was good, but held back output per hour.
5. **Slower technology diffusion.** The gap between frontier and laggard firms, mentioned above, appears particularly wide in Britain.
6. **Regional imbalances.** Outside London and the South East, UK cities tend to be less productive than comparable cities in other European countries, possibly reflecting weaker transport links and fewer high-skilled jobs.
7. **Public investment and planning.** Years of relatively low public investment, and a planning system that makes building homes, labs and infrastructure slow and costly, are increasingly cited as brakes on growth.

The puzzle has real consequences. Weak productivity is a big part of why real wages in the UK grew so little in the 2010s, and why the public finances have been under such pressure: slower growth means less tax revenue for any given set of tax rates.

## The trouble with measuring productivity

Productivity statistics are less solid than they look. Output is easy to count when a factory produces cars, but much harder for services, which make up around four-fifths of the UK economy.

- **Public services.** How do you measure the output of a hospital or a school? For a long time, statisticians assumed public sector output simply equalled its inputs, meaning productivity could never grow. The ONS now uses measures such as treatments delivered and pupil attainment, but these remain imperfect.
- **Quality changes.** A smartphone today does far more than one from a decade ago at a similar price. If statisticians underestimate quality improvements, they will underestimate real output and productivity.
- **Free digital goods.** Search engines, maps and video calls are free to users, so they barely appear in GDP, yet they clearly add to welfare. Some economists argue that mismeasurement explains part of the productivity slowdown. Most studies conclude it explains only a small part, not least because the slowdown was widespread across industries and countries, including those that make little use of such services.
- **Hours worked.** Estimating hours relies on surveys, which have their own problems. The UK's Labour Force Survey, a key source, has suffered from falling response rates since 2023.

Measurement problems are a reason for caution, not for dismissing the data altogether.

## Worked example: the computer age and the productivity paradox

In 1987, Robert Solow wrote: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Despite decades of investment in computers, US productivity growth had been sluggish since the early 1970s.

Then, from about 1995 to 2004, US productivity growth roughly doubled, to around 3% a year. Economists traced much of the improvement to information technology: first in the industries making computers and chips, then increasingly in industries that used them, such as retail and wholesale. Walmart's use of IT to manage logistics and inventory was a widely studied example.

The lesson many drew, building on work by the economic historian Paul David on the electric dynamo, was that general-purpose technologies take a long time to pay off. Factories electrified in the late nineteenth century, but productivity gains came only decades later, once firms redesigned buildings and workflows around electric motors rather than simply replacing steam engines. Technology needs **complementary investments**, in skills, organisation and processes, before its benefits show up in the figures.

The IT boom then faded in the mid-2000s, well before the financial crisis, and productivity growth across rich countries has been modest ever since.

## Could AI be the next wave?

Which brings us to artificial intelligence. Since the release of powerful generative AI tools from late 2022, there has been intense debate about whether they could revive productivity growth.

Optimists point to studies showing large gains in specific tasks. Experiments with customer service agents, software developers and professional writers found that AI assistance helped workers complete tasks faster, often with the biggest improvements for less experienced staff. If such gains spread across the economy, some forecasts suggest productivity growth could rise by a percentage point or more a year for a decade.

Sceptics urge caution. Daron Acemoglu, a Nobel laureate, estimated in 2024 that AI might add only around 0.7% to total factor productivity over a decade, because only a limited share of tasks can be profitably automated or substantially improved in the near term. Others note the lesson of the computer age: big gains may require years of reorganisation, and early results from controlled experiments do not always translate into economy-wide effects.

There are also questions about distribution. Productivity gains raise the size of the pie, but whether workers share in them depends on competition, bargaining power and policy. As of 2026, the honest answer is that it is too early to tell whether AI will prove a transformation, a modest boost, or something in between.

## Key takeaways

- Productivity, usually measured as output per hour, is the main driver of long-run living standards; Krugman's line that "in the long run it is almost everything" is not much of an exaggeration.
- Growth comes from more capital per worker, better skills and total factor productivity, the efficiency with which they are combined, which matters most over time.
- UK productivity growth fell from around 2% a year before 2008 to roughly a quarter of that afterwards, with weak investment, slow diffusion of technology and regional gaps among the leading explanations.
- Measuring productivity is hard, especially for services, public sector output and free digital goods, but mismeasurement probably explains only a small part of the slowdown.
- AI could become the next general-purpose technology, but history suggests gains may take years to appear and estimates of their size vary widely.
