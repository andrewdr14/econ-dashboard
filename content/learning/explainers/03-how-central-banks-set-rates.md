Eight times a year, nine people gather in a room at the Bank of England in Threadneedle Street and vote on a single number. Their decision, often a change of just a quarter of a percentage point, will affect mortgage bills in Manchester, savings accounts in Swansea, the value of the pound in Tokyo and, eventually, the prices on supermarket shelves. Similar meetings take place in Washington, Frankfurt and dozens of other capitals.

It sounds remarkably simple. Yet behind that one number lies a long chain of cause and effect, a great deal of uncertainty, and an awkward fact that shapes everything central bankers do: the full effect of their decisions takes something like 18 months to two years to arrive. They are steering a supertanker while looking at where it was, not where it is going.

## Who sets the rate, and which rate

Each major central bank has a **policy rate**, the interest rate it directly controls.

- **The Bank of England** sets **Bank Rate** through its nine-member **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**, which meets eight times a year. The government gives it a 2% CPI inflation target.
- **The US Federal Reserve** sets a target range for the **federal funds rate**, the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. Decisions are made by the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**, also meeting eight times a year. Its mandate from Congress is "maximum employment" and "stable prices", which it interprets as 2% inflation on the PCE measure.
- **The European Central Bank (ECB)** sets three rates for the euro area, of which the **deposit facility rate** is now the main signal. Decisions are made by its Governing Council, which includes the heads of the national central banks of euro countries. Its target is 2% inflation over the medium term.

## How a central bank makes its rate stick

A central bank cannot simply order every bank to charge a certain rate. Instead it uses its unique position as the bank for banks.

Commercial banks hold accounts, called **reserves**, at the central bank. These are the ultimate form of money that banks use to settle payments with each other. The central bank decides what interest it pays on those reserves. In the UK, reserves are remunerated at Bank Rate. If Bank Rate rises to 5%, no bank will lend to another bank overnight for much less than 5%, because it could earn 5% risk-free by leaving the money at the Bank of England. The Fed and ECB use similar mechanisms.

So the policy rate sets the floor for the cost of very short-term money. From there it spreads outwards to everything else.

## The transmission channels

The route from the policy rate to inflation is called the **monetary transmission mechanism**. It runs through several channels at once.

### Borrowing and saving

Higher policy rates feed through to the rates banks charge on loans and pay on deposits. Borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more rewarding. Households postpone buying cars or extending kitchens; businesses shelve investment projects. Spending falls, and with it the pressure on prices.

How fast this happens depends on the structure of borrowing. In the UK, most mortgages are now fixed for two or five years, so a rate rise hits households in waves as fixed deals expire. In the US, the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is standard, so many homeowners are largely insulated, though new buyers are not. In parts of the euro area, such as Spain and Italy, variable-rate mortgages have historically been more common, so changes pass through faster.

### Asset prices and wealth

Higher interest rates tend to lower the prices of assets such as shares, bonds and houses, because future income streams are worth less when you can earn more by holding cash. People who feel poorer tend to spend less.

### The exchange rate

If a central bank raises rates relative to other countries, its currency usually becomes more attractive to investors and rises in value. A stronger currency makes imports cheaper, which directly lowers inflation, and makes exports dearer, which cools demand. For open economies like the UK, this channel matters a great deal.

### Expectations

Perhaps the most powerful channel is psychological. If firms and workers believe the central bank will do whatever is needed to bring inflation back to target, they will set prices and negotiate pay accordingly. Inflation expectations become self-fulfilling in a helpful way. If they stop believing it, expectations can drift upwards and become self-fulfilling in an unhelpful one.

## Why it takes 18 months to two years

Milton Friedman famously described monetary policy as acting with "long and variable lags". The reasons are not hard to see once you trace the chain.

1. Market interest rates often move as soon as a rise is expected, sometimes before it is even announced.
2. But households and businesses adjust slowly. Fixed-rate mortgages roll over gradually. Investment plans already under way continue.
3. Lower spending then has to feed through into weaker hiring and slower wage growth.
4. Only after that do firms ease off on price increases.

The Bank of England has long said that the peak impact of a rate change on inflation comes after roughly 18 to 24 months, though estimates vary and the lags may have changed as mortgage markets have shifted. This is why central banks talk about **forecasts** constantly. They set policy for where inflation is likely to be in two years' time, not where it is today. It also explains why they are often accused of acting too late on the way up and too late on the way down.

## Rules of thumb: the Taylor rule

How should a central bank decide what rate to set? In 1993, the Stanford economist John Taylor proposed a simple formula that described the Fed's behaviour surprisingly well. In plain English, the **Taylor rule** says:

> Set the interest rate at a "neutral" level, then raise it when inflation is above target or the economy is running hot, and cut it when inflation is below target or the economy is weak.

The crucial detail is that the rate should rise by **more** than inflation does. If inflation goes up by one percentage point and the central bank raises rates by only half a point, the **real interest rate** (the interest rate minus inflation) has actually fallen, which stimulates the economy and adds fuel to the fire. Responding more than one-for-one is known as the **Taylor principle**.

A worked example: suppose the neutral real rate is 1%, inflation is 4% against a 2% target, and the economy is running at full capacity. A simple Taylor rule would suggest a policy rate of about 1% (neutral) plus 4% (inflation) plus half the 2-point overshoot, giving roughly 6%.

No central bank follows a rule mechanically. The neutral rate cannot be observed directly, estimates of spare capacity are uncertain, and shocks vary. But Taylor-style rules are a useful benchmark, and they are often used to ask whether policy looks unusually tight or loose.

## Beyond the policy rate

### Forward guidance

Since central banks act through expectations, what they say can matter as much as what they do. **Forward guidance** means signalling the likely path of future rates. In August 2013, the Bank of England said it did not expect to raise rates at least until unemployment had fallen to 7%. The most famous example of words moving markets came from the ECB's Mario Draghi in July 2012, at the height of the euro crisis, when he said the ECB was ready to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro. Bond markets calmed almost immediately.

### Quantitative easing

When rates hit zero after 2008, central banks turned to **quantitative easing (QE)**: creating central bank money to buy government bonds and other assets, in order to push down longer-term interest rates. Reversing it, by selling bonds or letting them mature, is known as **quantitative tightening**.

## Why independence

Most advanced economies now leave interest rates to central banks that are independent of day-to-day politics. The Bank of England gained operational independence in 1997. The ECB's independence is written into the EU treaties. The Fed's independence rests on law and long convention.

The argument for this arrangement is about time horizons. Politicians face an obvious temptation to cut rates before elections, enjoying the boost to growth and leaving the inflation to arrive later. Knowing this, people may expect higher inflation in any case, which makes it harder to control. Handing the job to unelected officials with a clear target makes promises to keep inflation low more believable. The cautionary tale often cited is the US in the early 1970s, when Fed Chair Arthur Burns came under heavy pressure from President Nixon to keep policy loose; inflation surged during the decade.

The case against, or at least for caution, is democratic. Central bankers make decisions with huge distributional consequences, for borrowers and savers, renters and homeowners, without facing voters. QE in particular raised asset prices, benefiting those who already owned assets. Critics argue that this blurs the line between monetary policy and fiscal policy, which belongs to elected governments. Defenders reply that independence comes with accountability: published targets, minutes, forecasts and regular testimony to parliaments.

## A real-world episode: the 2021-24 cycle

The post-pandemic inflation surge was the sharpest test of modern central banking. As inflation started climbing in 2021, many central banks initially judged it "transitory", driven by reopening bottlenecks that would fade. That judgement proved too hopeful, and they were later widely criticised for moving late.

Once they moved, they moved fast. The Bank of England began raising rates in December 2021, from 0.1%, and reached 5.25% by August 2023. The Fed lifted its target range from near zero in March 2022 to 5.25-5.5% by July 2023, including several unusually large rises of three quarters of a percentage point. The ECB raised its deposit rate from minus 0.5% in July 2022 to 4% by September 2023.

The lags then played out much as the textbook predicts. Inflation fell substantially during 2023, helped by easing energy prices, while the squeeze on UK households built gradually as fixed-rate mortgages expired and were refinanced at much higher rates. Rate cuts began in 2024: the ECB in June, the Bank of England in August and the Fed in September.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The central bank sets mortgage rates."** It sets a short-term rate. Fixed mortgage rates depend mainly on market expectations of future rates, which is why they can move before the central bank does.
- **"Raising rates fights inflation by making things more expensive."** Higher borrowing costs do add to some bills, but the mechanism is reduced demand, which eventually slows price rises across the economy.
- **"If inflation is high today, rates should be raised today until it falls."** Because of lags, a central bank reacting only to current inflation risks overshooting badly in the other direction.
- **"Central banks print money to fund governments."** QE involves creating money to buy bonds in the market, but independent central banks do so for monetary policy purposes and are generally barred from financing governments directly.

## Key takeaways

- Central banks control a short-term policy rate by setting the interest paid on banks' reserves; it then spreads to other borrowing costs.
- Policy works through borrowing, asset prices, the exchange rate and, crucially, expectations.
- Effects build slowly, peaking roughly 18 to 24 months later, so policy is set on forecasts rather than today's inflation.
- The Taylor rule offers a useful benchmark: raise rates more than one-for-one with inflation.
- Independence aims to make low-inflation promises credible, but it raises genuine questions about democratic accountability.
