If you have ever come back from a holiday abroad and noticed that your spending money went further this year than last, or not nearly as far, you have felt an exchange rate move. Currency markets are the largest financial markets on Earth: according to the Bank for International Settlements, foreign exchange trading averaged about 7.5 trillion dollars a day in 2022. Behind every one of those trades is a simple question: how much of one currency should you get for another? The answer depends on a tangle of forces, from interest rates and inflation to trade flows, investor fear and, occasionally, the deliberate hand of a government. None of them works alone, and none predicts the next day's move reliably. But together they explain a great deal about why currencies rise and fall over months and years.

## First, some vocabulary

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. If £1 buys $1.30, the pound-dollar rate is 1.30. When that number rises, the pound has **appreciated** (strengthened) against the dollar; when it falls, the pound has **depreciated** (weakened).

Economists distinguish the **nominal** exchange rate, the number you see on the screen, from the **real** exchange rate, which adjusts for differences in prices between countries. If the pound falls 10% against the euro but British prices rise 10% faster than euro area prices, British goods are no more competitive than before: the real exchange rate has not moved.

There is also the **effective** exchange rate, a weighted average of a currency against all its main trading partners. It is often more meaningful than any single bilateral rate. The pound can fall against the dollar while rising against the euro.

## Interest rate differentials

The single most powerful short-term driver of exchange rates is the difference between interest rates in different countries.

Imagine a pension fund with money to park for a year. If UK interest rates are 5% and euro area rates are 3%, holding pounds looks more attractive, other things being equal. As investors buy pounds to take advantage, the pound tends to rise. When the Bank of England unexpectedly raises rates, or signals it will keep them higher for longer, the pound typically strengthens within minutes.

There is a subtlety. Economic theory, in the form of **uncovered interest parity**, suggests the higher-yielding currency should be expected to *fall* over time by roughly the interest rate gap, so that investors earn the same return wherever they put their money. In practice this relationship often fails over months and even years. High-yielding currencies have frequently held their value or kept rising, which is why the **carry trade**, borrowing in a low-interest currency such as the yen to invest in a higher-yielding one, has been profitable for long stretches. It has also produced sudden, painful reversals when markets panic and investors rush to unwind their positions at once.

What matters most is not the level of rates but *surprises* relative to what markets expected. A rate rise that was fully anticipated may move a currency very little.

## Purchasing power parity: the long-run anchor

Over longer horizons, prices start to matter. The idea of **purchasing power parity** (PPP) is that, in the long run, exchange rates should move to equalise the price of the same basket of goods in different countries. If a basket costs £100 in London and $150 in New York, the "fair" exchange rate would be $1.50 per pound.

The Economist magazine has popularised a light-hearted version since 1986: the Big Mac index, which compares the price of a McDonald's burger around the world. It is a joke with a serious point. Currencies can stray far from PPP for years. Many goods and almost all services, such as haircuts and rent, are not traded across borders. Taxes, transport costs and market power all get in the way.

Still, PPP has some pull. Countries with persistently higher inflation tend to see their currencies weaken over time. The Turkish lira and the Argentine peso, both of which suffered very high inflation in the early 2020s, lost a large share of their value against the dollar.

> Interest rates move currencies over days and months; inflation and productivity tend to win out over years and decades.

## The current account and capital flows

A country's **current account** records its trade in goods and services plus income flows such as dividends. A country that imports much more than it exports runs a current account deficit, and it has to finance that deficit by attracting foreign capital: selling assets, bonds or companies to foreigners.

As long as foreign investors are happy to provide that money, the currency can hold up. The UK has run current account deficits most years for decades, financed by inflows into its financial markets and property. But a large deficit leaves a currency vulnerable if investors' mood sours. In 2016, the Bank of England governor Mark Carney warned that the UK relied on "the kindness of strangers", a phrase he borrowed from Tennessee Williams.

In today's world, cross-border flows of capital dwarf trade flows. A single decision by global investors to move money into or out of a country's bonds can matter more for its currency than a year's trade figures.

## Risk appetite and safe havens

Currencies also respond to the global mood. In calm, optimistic times, investors seek higher returns in riskier assets, which often helps currencies of commodity exporters and emerging economies, such as the Australian dollar or the Brazilian real.

In times of fear, money tends to flow into **safe havens**: currencies seen as stores of value in a storm. The traditional safe havens are:

- **The US dollar**, because of the depth of US financial markets and its role as the world's main reserve currency. Many global debts and trade contracts are priced in dollars, so when trouble hits, everyone needs them.
- **The Swiss franc**, backed by Switzerland's political stability, low inflation and large foreign assets.
- **The Japanese yen**, because Japan is a large creditor to the rest of the world and Japanese investors tend to bring money home in a crisis.

This is why the dollar often strengthens during global crises, even ones that start in America, as it did in 2008. That reputation is not guaranteed forever. In the first half of 2025, amid sweeping US tariff announcements and concerns about policy uncertainty, the dollar fell noticeably against most major currencies, prompting debate about whether its safe-haven status was eroding. Whether that proves a blip or a turning point is still unclear.

## When governments step in

### Intervention

Central banks and finance ministries can buy or sell their own currency in the market. Japan intervened to support the yen in 2022 and again in 2024, after it had weakened to its lowest levels against the dollar in decades. Such intervention can be effective in the short run, especially if it surprises markets, but it rarely reverses a trend driven by large interest rate differences unless policy changes too.

### Pegs and floats

Countries choose very different exchange rate regimes.

| Regime | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Free float | Market sets the rate; central bank focuses on inflation | UK, US, euro area, Japan |
| Managed float | Market sets the rate, with frequent intervention | Many emerging economies |
| Peg | Currency fixed to another, defended by the central bank | Hong Kong dollar to US dollar (since 1983) |
| Currency union | Countries share a single currency | The euro |

A **peg** offers stability for trade and can import credibility from a trusted central bank, but it means giving up independent monetary policy: interest rates must follow those of the anchor currency. Denmark has kept the krone pegged to the euro, and before that to the Deutschmark, for decades.

Pegs can break dramatically. On 16 September 1992, "Black Wednesday", the UK was forced out of the European Exchange Rate Mechanism after spending billions of pounds and raising interest rates in a futile attempt to defend sterling against speculators. In January 2015, the Swiss National Bank abruptly abandoned the floor of 1.20 francs per euro it had held since 2011, and the franc jumped by roughly 20% within minutes.

## Worked example: sterling after the Brexit vote

On the night of 23 to 24 June 2016, as the results of the EU referendum came in, the pound fell from around $1.50 to below $1.35, one of its largest one-day moves in modern times. It continued to slide in the following months.

Several of the forces above were at work at once. Markets expected slower UK growth and therefore lower interest rates; the Bank of England did indeed cut rates that August. Uncertainty about the UK's future trading relationship raised the risk premium investors demanded for holding British assets. And with a large current account deficit to finance, the UK was exposed to any slowdown in capital inflows.

The weaker pound then had visible effects. Imported goods became more expensive, and UK inflation rose to around 3% by late 2017, squeezing real wages. Exporters and companies earning revenues abroad, including many in the FTSE 100, saw a boost to their sterling profits. Tourists visiting Britain found it cheaper. It was a vivid illustration of how a currency move creates winners and losers.

## Is a weaker currency good or bad?

This is one of the most common questions, and the honest answer is: it depends who you are.

A weaker currency **helps**:

- **Exporters**, whose goods become cheaper abroad.
- **Domestic firms competing with imports**, which face less foreign competition.
- **Tourism**, as the country becomes cheaper to visit.
- **Holders of foreign assets**, whose investments are worth more in domestic currency.

A weaker currency **hurts**:

- **Consumers**, who pay more for imports, including food and energy priced in dollars.
- **Firms relying on imported parts or raw materials.**
- **Those with debts in foreign currencies**, a serious danger for many emerging economies.
- **Holidaymakers abroad**, whose money buys less.

It also tends to push up inflation, which may force the central bank to keep interest rates higher than it otherwise would. And because exports and imports take time to respond to price changes, the trade benefits often arrive slowly, while the price rises come quickly.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A strong currency means a strong economy."** Not necessarily. Switzerland has prospered with a strong franc and Germany with a relatively cheap euro. A currency's level is a price, not a scorecard.
- **"Exchange rates are predictable from economic data."** A famous 1983 study by Richard Meese and Kenneth Rogoff found that standard models struggled to beat a simple guess of "no change" over short horizons. That finding has proved remarkably durable.
- **"Governments can set the rate wherever they like."** Only by sacrificing control over interest rates, or by holding enormous reserves, and even then pegs can break.

## Key takeaways

- Interest rate differences, and especially surprises about future rates, are the strongest short-term drivers of exchange rates.
- Over long periods, relative inflation and purchasing power parity pull currencies towards a rough "fair value", though they can stray far from it for years.
- Current account deficits make a currency more dependent on foreign capital, while global fear sends money towards safe havens such as the dollar, Swiss franc and yen.
- Pegs offer stability at the cost of monetary independence, and can break suddenly, as in 1992 and 2015.
- A weaker currency helps exporters and hurts importers and consumers; whether it is "good" depends on where you stand.
