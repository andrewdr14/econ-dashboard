In the early 1930s, as the US economy collapsed, policymakers faced an astonishing problem: nobody knew how big the economy was, or how fast it was shrinking. They had scraps of data on freight car loadings, steel output and stock prices, but no overall measure. The US Congress asked a young economist, Simon Kuznets, to fill the gap. His estimates of national income, first published in 1934, laid the foundations for what we now call **gross domestic product**, or GDP.

Almost a century later, GDP is everywhere. It decides whether an economy is officially in recession, frames budgets and election campaigns, and sits behind almost every international comparison. Yet Kuznets himself warned that a nation's welfare could scarcely be inferred from a measure of its income. To use GDP well, you need to know both what it is and what it was never meant to be.

## What GDP is

GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders over a period, usually a quarter or a year.

Each part of that definition does work:

- **Market value** means things are added up using prices, so a car counts for more than a cup of coffee.
- **Final** means we only count goods at the end of the chain. The flour sold to a baker is not counted separately from the bread, otherwise we would count the flour twice.
- **Produced** means newly made. Selling a second-hand house does not add to GDP (though the estate agent's fee does).
- **Within a country's borders** means it does not matter who owns the factory. A Japanese-owned car plant in Sunderland counts towards UK GDP.

## Three ways to measure the same thing

One of the elegant features of national accounting is that GDP can be measured in three ways which, in principle, give the same answer. Every pound spent is a pound earned by someone, and corresponds to something produced.

1. **The output approach** adds up the **value added** at each stage of production: the value of what each business sells minus what it bought from other businesses. A baker who buys £60 of flour and sells £100 of bread has added £40.
2. **The income approach** adds up everything earned from production: wages and salaries, company profits, and income of the self-employed.
3. **The expenditure approach** adds up spending on final goods and services. This gives the famous equation: **GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)**, meaning household consumption, plus business investment, plus government spending, plus exports minus imports.

In practice, the three measures never match perfectly, because they come from different surveys and tax records. Statisticians reconcile them into a single best estimate. In the UK, early quarterly estimates lean heavily on the output measure, because monthly business surveys make it the quickest to produce.

## Real versus nominal

If an economy produces exactly the same goods this year as last year, but prices rise by 5%, the money value of output rises by 5%. That is **nominal GDP**. It tells you nothing about whether the economy is making more stuff.

So statisticians also produce **real GDP**, which strips out price changes using a measure called the **GDP deflator**. When people talk about "economic growth", they almost always mean growth in real GDP. Nominal GDP still matters, though: tax revenues and debts are in money terms, so ratios such as debt-to-GDP use nominal figures.

## Quarterly, annualised and the recession rule of thumb

Here is a trap for anyone reading international headlines. The UK and the euro area report quarterly growth as a simple quarter-on-quarter percentage. The US reports quarterly growth **annualised**, meaning the rate the economy would grow over a year if that quarter's pace persisted.

The difference is large. A quarter-on-quarter rise of 0.5% is roughly 2% annualised. So "the US grew 2%" and "the UK grew 0.5%" may describe exactly the same pace. Always check which convention is in use before comparing.

A common rule of thumb defines a **recession** as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. Many European statisticians and journalists use it. In the US, recessions are officially dated by a committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research, which weighs a broader range of data, including employment and incomes, and does not apply a strict two-quarter rule.

## Revisions: the first draft of history

GDP figures are estimates, and first estimates are based on incomplete data. As tax returns, annual business surveys and other sources arrive, the numbers are revised, sometimes for years afterwards.

Revisions can change the story. In 2023, the ONS revised its estimates of UK growth during the pandemic recovery. Before the revision, the UK appeared to be the only G7 economy still smaller than before the pandemic. Afterwards, UK output was estimated to have regained its pre-pandemic size by the end of 2021, far earlier than previously thought. Debates that had been conducted for months turned out to rest on a figure that no longer existed.

The lesson: treat the first estimate of any quarter as provisional, and be wary of drawing big conclusions from differences of a tenth of a percentage point.

## Per person, and other adjustments

A larger population usually means more output, which does not necessarily mean people are better off. **GDP per capita** (per person) is a better guide to living standards. The distinction has mattered in the UK since the late 2010s. In some recent years total GDP edged up while GDP per capita fell, because the population grew faster than output.

For comparisons between countries, economists also adjust for **purchasing power parity (PPP)**, which accounts for the fact that a pound or a dollar buys more in some countries than others. A haircut in Delhi costs far less than one in London, so converting at market exchange rates understates incomes in poorer countries.

Sometimes GDP is simply the wrong frame. The textbook case is Ireland. In 2015, Irish GDP was reported to have grown by about a quarter in a single year, after multinational companies moved valuable intellectual property, such as patents, onto Irish balance sheets. Nobody in Ireland was suddenly a quarter richer. The episode, nicknamed "leprechaun economics", led Ireland's statisticians to create an alternative measure, **modified gross national income**, often written GNI-star, which strips out many of these multinational effects.

## What GDP misses

GDP measures market production. That is its job, and it does it reasonably well. The trouble comes when it is treated as a measure of everything that matters.

### Unpaid work

Cooking, cleaning, childcare and caring for elderly relatives create enormous value, but because no money changes hands, they are not counted. An old economics joke observes that if a man marries his housekeeper, GDP falls, though nothing about the work has changed. Since unpaid domestic work is disproportionately done by women, GDP also understates women's economic contribution. Statistical agencies, including the ONS, publish separate "satellite accounts" estimating the value of household production, and it turns out to be very large.

### Distribution

GDP is a total. It says nothing about who gets what. An economy where all the gains from growth go to the top tenth of earners can post the same GDP growth as one where gains are widely shared. Median household income, or measures of inequality such as the Gini coefficient, are needed to tell those stories apart.

### The environment

GDP counts the timber sold from a felled forest but not the loss of the forest. It counts the output of a polluting factory, and then counts again the spending needed to clean up the pollution. Running down natural resources can boost GDP today while leaving the country poorer tomorrow. The "gross" in GDP is a clue: it makes no deduction for the wearing out of capital, whether machines or ecosystems. **Net domestic product**, which subtracts depreciation of physical capital, is a partial fix.

### Quality, free goods and the oddities

GDP struggles with things that are free at the point of use, such as search engines, online maps and many apps, and with quality improvements, such as a smartphone that does far more than its price suggests. Meanwhile, some things count that you might not expect. Since 2014, following European accounting rules, UK GDP has included estimates for illegal drugs and prostitution, on the grounds that these are market transactions like any other.

## Alternatives and complements

Nobody serious proposes abolishing GDP. Instead, there has been a long effort to put it alongside other measures.

- **The Human Development Index**, published by the UN Development Programme since 1990, combines income with life expectancy and education.
- **The Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission**, set up by the French government and reporting in 2009, argued for shifting emphasis from measuring production to measuring wellbeing, and for paying more attention to household income, distribution and sustainability.
- **National wellbeing programmes**: the ONS has published measures of life satisfaction and other aspects of wellbeing since the early 2010s, and New Zealand framed its 2019 budget around wellbeing goals. Bhutan has long promoted "Gross National Happiness".
- **Green accounting** attempts to value natural capital and its depletion, though putting prices on ecosystems is fraught.

Critics of these alternatives make fair points. Composite indices involve arbitrary weightings. Wellbeing surveys can be hard to interpret over time. And GDP has one huge virtue: it is a consistent, well-understood and internationally comparable measure. Higher GDP per capita is also strongly associated, across countries, with longer lives, better health and more resources to spend on everything from hospitals to environmental protection. The most common position among economists is that GDP is indispensable but insufficient.

## Common misconceptions

- **"GDP measures how well off we are."** It measures output. Living standards depend on output per person, its distribution, and many things outside the market.
- **"GDP growth is the same as people's incomes rising."** Not necessarily. Population growth, rising profits abroad, or changes in prices of imports and exports can all drive a wedge between GDP and household income.
- **"Government spending is just added to GDP, so more spending always means more growth."** Government spending is part of GDP by definition, but whether extra spending raises total output depends on how it affects other spending and on how the economy responds over time.
- **"The first estimate is the truth."** It is a first draft, and revisions can be substantial.

## Key takeaways

- GDP measures the market value of final goods and services produced in a country, and can be calculated from output, incomes or spending.
- Real GDP strips out inflation; per-capita GDP adjusts for population; the US annualises quarterly growth while the UK and euro area do not.
- Early estimates are often revised, sometimes enough to change the story.
- GDP misses unpaid work, distribution, environmental damage and much of what makes life good.
- Most economists see GDP as indispensable but insufficient, best read alongside measures of income, inequality and wellbeing.
