"Neoliberalism" is one of those words that tells you as much about the speaker as about the subject. For some, it names the ideology that broke the grip of 1970s stagnation, freed economies from heavy-handed control and helped lift hundreds of millions of people out of poverty. For others, it names the ideology that hollowed out industrial towns, widened inequality and set the stage for the 2008 financial crisis. For still others, it is a vague insult so stretched that it no longer means anything at all.

All three groups have a point, which is what makes the subject worth understanding properly. This explainer sets out where the ideas came from, how they were put into practice, what the evidence suggests about the results, and why the term remains so contested.

## What the word means

Broadly, **neoliberalism** refers to a set of ideas and policies that favour markets, competition, private enterprise, free trade and the free movement of capital, alongside a smaller role for the state in directly running the economy. Typical policies include **privatisation** (selling state-owned companies), **deregulation** (removing or loosening rules on business), lower taxes on income and profits, independent central banks focused on low inflation, weaker trade union power, and openness to international trade and investment.

The "neo" distinguishes it from the **classical liberalism** of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, associated with Adam Smith and the free-trade movement. Note a source of endless confusion: in Europe, "liberal" usually means pro-market, while in the US it usually means left of centre. Neoliberalism refers to the European sense.

An important nuance is that many neoliberal thinkers did not want a weak state. They wanted a strong state with a different job: enforcing contracts and property rights, maintaining sound money, upholding competition and building the legal framework within which markets could work. That distinguishes neoliberalism from pure libertarianism or laissez-faire.

## Origins: a movement of ideas

### The 1930s and 1940s

The intellectual movement began as a reaction to the crises of the 1930s, when many people concluded that capitalism had failed and that economic planning, whether fascist, communist or democratic, was the future. In 1938, a group of liberal intellectuals met in Paris at what became known as the Walter Lippmann Colloquium, named after the American journalist whose book had prompted it. It was there that the term "neoliberalism" was adopted for a renewed liberalism suited to modern conditions.

The Austrian economist **Friedrich Hayek** became the movement's leading figure. His 1944 book *The Road to Serfdom* argued that central economic planning, even when well-intentioned, concentrates power and tends to erode personal and political freedom. Hayek also made a deeper argument about knowledge: the information needed to run an economy is dispersed among millions of people, and market prices are a way of coordinating it that no planner could replicate.

In 1947, Hayek convened a meeting at Mont Pèlerin in Switzerland. The resulting **Mont Pelerin Society** brought together economists, philosophers and historians, including Milton Friedman, Ludwig von Mises, Karl Popper and George Stigler. It was a small, often quarrelsome network, but it would prove extraordinarily influential.

### Chicago and Freiburg

Two schools gave neoliberal ideas much of their shape. The **Chicago school**, centred on Friedman and Stigler at the University of Chicago, championed free markets, monetarism and scepticism of government regulation, arguing that regulators were often "captured" by the industries they oversaw.

In Germany, the **ordoliberals** of the Freiburg school, such as Walter Eucken, argued that the state must actively create and police competitive markets. Their ideas influenced Ludwig Erhard and West Germany's post-war **social market economy**, which combined competitive markets with a substantial welfare state. The German strand is a reminder that neoliberalism was never a single doctrine.

For decades, these were dissenting voices in a Keynesian world. A network of think tanks, such as the Institute of Economic Affairs, founded in London in 1955, kept the ideas in circulation. Hayek's Nobel prize in 1974 and Friedman's in 1976 signalled that the tide was turning.

## From ideas to power

The economic crises of the 1970s, with stagflation, strikes and energy shocks, created the opening. The post-war consensus seemed exhausted, and neoliberal thinkers had alternatives ready.

### Britain and America

Margaret Thatcher, elected in 1979, and Ronald Reagan, elected in 1980, became the political faces of the movement. Their governments did many of the same things, though not all.

- **Taxes**: the UK top income tax rate fell from 83% to 60% in 1979, and to 40% in 1988. The US top rate fell from 70% to 28% by 1988.
- **Privatisation**: the UK sold British Telecom (1984), British Gas (1986), water companies (1989) and much else. Council tenants gained the right to buy their homes from 1980.
- **Deregulation**: the 1986 "Big Bang" transformed the City of London; the US deregulated airlines, trucking and parts of finance.
- **Labour markets**: both governments confronted trade unions, Thatcher in the 1984-85 miners' strike and Reagan in the 1981 air traffic controllers' strike.

### Not only the right

Neoliberal reforms were not the property of one party. In New Zealand, a Labour government after 1984 carried out sweeping liberalisation, nicknamed "Rogernomics" after finance minister Roger Douglas. Australia's Labor governments floated the currency and cut tariffs. In the 1990s, Bill Clinton declared that "the era of big government is over" and Tony Blair's New Labour kept much of the Thatcher settlement while handing interest rate decisions to the Bank of England.

There was a darker chapter too. In Chile, after the 1973 military coup, economists trained in Chicago, the so-called "Chicago Boys", carried out free-market reforms under the Pinochet dictatorship. Critics see this as proof that neoliberalism could coexist with repression; defenders note that Hayek and Friedman's involvement was limited and argue that Chile's later democratic governments kept the market framework and enjoyed strong growth.

### The Washington Consensus and globalisation

In 1989, the economist John Williamson coined the term **"Washington Consensus"** for ten policy reforms that institutions in Washington, such as the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury, broadly agreed Latin American countries needed: fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, secure property rights and so on. Williamson later complained that the phrase had become shorthand for "market fundamentalism", which he said was not what he meant.

After the fall of the Berlin Wall, former communist countries undertook rapid market reforms, sometimes called "shock therapy". Trade barriers fell worldwide, the World Trade Organization was founded in 1995, and China joined it in 2001. Capital flowed freely across borders on an unprecedented scale. This is what people usually mean by **globalisation**.

## The evidence on outcomes

Judging the results is genuinely difficult, because neoliberal policies were mixed with many other forces: technology, the rise of China, demographics and the end of the Cold War. Still, some findings are widely accepted.

**Points in favour.** Inflation, the scourge of the 1970s, was brought under control across the rich world and stayed low for decades. Some privatised industries, such as UK telecoms, became more efficient and innovative. Global trade expanded hugely. Extreme poverty worldwide fell dramatically: World Bank estimates suggest the share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from around a third in 1990 to about a tenth by 2015. India's liberalising reforms of 1991 were followed by much faster growth.

**Points against.** Inequality rose sharply within many rich countries, notably the UK in the 1980s and the US over several decades. Growth in Western economies was not obviously faster than in the more regulated post-war decades. Financial deregulation was followed by repeated crises, culminating in 2008. Russia's 1990s transition was accompanied by economic collapse and the rise of oligarchs. Some privatisations, such as English water, remain bitterly controversial over debt, investment and sewage spills. Latin American growth under Washington Consensus policies in the 1990s was widely judged disappointing.

**Points that cut both ways.** The biggest poverty reductions came in China and other East Asian economies, which opened up to markets and trade but did so gradually and with heavy state direction. Both sides claim them as evidence.

Even the IMF, often seen as neoliberalism's enforcer, published a notable article in 2016 titled "Neoliberalism: Oversold?". Its authors, Jonathan Ostry, Prakash Loungani and Davide Furceri, argued that free trade and foreign direct investment had delivered real benefits, but that unrestricted capital flows and austerity had sometimes increased inequality and instability without delivering the promised growth.

## The critiques

### From the left

Critics on the left argue that neoliberalism shifted power from workers to capital and from the public to private interests. The geographer David Harvey, in *A Brief History of Neoliberalism* (2005), framed it as a project to restore the power of economic elites. Others focus on stagnant wages for many workers, insecure employment, the financialisation of the economy, underinvestment in public services and the bank bailouts of 2008.

### From the right

Criticism also comes from conservatives. Some, often described as post-liberal or national conservatives, argue that market globalisation dissolved communities, families and national industries, and that free movement of capital and labour served corporations rather than citizens. They want more industrial policy and tighter borders.

### From free-marketeers themselves

Many economists who favour markets reject the label entirely. They point out that government spending as a share of GDP did not fall much in most rich countries, that regulation multiplied, and that a "neoliberal era" in which the state remained so large is something of a myth. Some argue the 2008 crisis owed as much to government failure, such as implicit guarantees for big banks, as to deregulation.

## Why the term is so contested

A 2009 study by political scientists Taylor Boas and Jordan Gans-Morse found that "neoliberalism" was used in academic writing overwhelmingly by critics, was rarely defined, and was almost never used by people to describe themselves. That pattern helps explain the frustration on both sides. Critics see it as an essential name for a real historical shift; defenders see it as a catch-all insult that lumps together Hayek, Pinochet, Clinton and the EU single market.

A fair reading might be this: there was a real, identifiable turn towards markets from the late 1970s, shaped by an organised intellectual movement. But it varied enormously across countries, and "neoliberalism" is more useful as a description of that broad turn than as a precise doctrine.

## After 2008 and 2016

The 2008 crisis brought bank bailouts, tougher financial regulation and years of ultra-low interest rates. The political shocks of 2016, the Brexit vote and Donald Trump's first election, were widely read as revolts against globalisation. Trade policy turned more protectionist, with US tariffs on China from 2018 retained under President Biden. Biden's administration passed large industrial policy measures in 2022, the CHIPS Act and Inflation Reduction Act, and in 2023 his national security adviser Jake Sullivan spoke of a "new Washington consensus". Trump's second term, from 2025, brought sweeping new tariffs.

Whether this marks the end of the neoliberal era, or merely a revision of it, is one of the live questions of current economics. Many core features, including independent central banks, broadly open markets and private ownership of most industry, remain firmly in place.

## Key takeaways

- Neoliberalism refers broadly to a turn towards markets, trade, privatisation and deregulation from the late 1970s, rooted in ideas developed by Hayek, Friedman and others from the 1930s onwards.
- It was implemented by governments of both right and left, in very different forms across countries.
- Its record is mixed: inflation was tamed and global poverty fell sharply, but inequality rose in many rich countries and financial crises recurred.
- The term is used mostly by critics, rarely by supporters, which is why it is so fiercely contested.
- Since 2008 and especially 2016, protectionism and industrial policy have returned, though many neoliberal institutions endure.
