Imagine a top barrister who also happens to be the fastest typist in her chambers. Should she type her own documents? She could do it more quickly than her clerk, yet almost everyone's instinct says no: an hour spent typing is an hour not spent in court, earning many times what the clerk is paid. She is better at both jobs, but she gains by sticking to the one where her edge is largest and paying someone else to do the other. That small piece of common sense, scaled up to whole nations, is one of the oldest and most surprising ideas in economics. It is called comparative advantage, and it sits underneath almost every argument for free trade made in the past two centuries.

## The idea Ricardo made famous

The principle is usually credited to David Ricardo, a London stockbroker turned economist, who set it out in *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* in 1817. (Robert Torrens had sketched similar thoughts a little earlier, and historians still argue about who got there first.)

Ricardo used two countries and two goods. In his example, England needed the labour of 100 men for a year to make a given quantity of cloth and 120 men to make a given quantity of wine. Portugal needed only 90 men for the cloth and 80 for the wine.

| Labour needed (men per year) | Cloth | Wine |
|---|---|---|
| England | 100 | 120 |
| Portugal | 90 | 80 |

Portugal is better at making both. In the jargon, it has an **absolute advantage** in each good. A naive reading says Portugal has nothing to gain from trading with England. Ricardo showed that this is wrong.

## Opportunity cost is the key

The trick is to stop comparing countries with each other and start comparing each country with itself. What matters is **opportunity cost**: what you give up to make something.

In England, producing one unit of wine uses labour that could have made 1.2 units of cloth (120 divided by 100). In Portugal, one unit of wine costs only about 0.89 units of cloth (80 divided by 90). So wine is relatively cheap to make in Portugal. Flip it round and cloth is relatively cheap in England: a unit of cloth costs England about 0.83 units of wine, but costs Portugal about 1.13.

Each country therefore has a **comparative advantage** in something. Portugal's is wine, where its lead is biggest. England's is cloth, where it is least behind. If each specialises in its comparative-advantage good and they swap at any price between their two internal trade-offs, say one unit of wine for one unit of cloth, both can end up consuming more of both goods than they could alone.

Work it through. Suppose England moves its 120 wine-makers into cloth: it now makes 2.2 units of cloth. Portugal moves its 90 cloth-makers into wine and makes 2.125 units of wine. Before trade the two countries together made 2 units of cloth and 2 units of wine. After specialisation they make 2.2 units of cloth and 2.125 units of wine, using exactly the same workers. The extra output is the **gain from trade**, and there is something left over for both sides however they divide it.

> Comparative advantage says a country gains by specialising in what it does *relatively* best, not what it does better than anyone else. Even a country that is worse at everything has something to offer.

## Why the idea is so often misunderstood

Paul Samuelson, the Nobel laureate, liked to say that comparative advantage was one of the few ideas in the social sciences that is both true and not obvious. The non-obviousness shows up in everyday argument.

- **"We can't compete with countries that are better at everything."** You don't need to. What determines trade is relative costs, and exchange rates and wages adjust so that every country ends up with something it can sell.
- **"Trade is a contest between nations."** Both partners can gain at once, just as you and your supermarket both gain when you buy groceries.
- **"Low-wage countries will undercut all our industries."** Low wages usually reflect low productivity. Richer countries pay more because their workers produce more per hour, and that is what lets them stay competitive in other lines of business.
- **"A trade deficit means we are losing."** A bilateral deficit with one country tells you little; you might run a deficit with your grocer and a surplus with your employer. Overall trade balances mostly reflect how much a country saves and invests, not how well it trades.

## Modern trade looks rather different

Ricardo's world was two countries swapping finished goods. Today much trade happens within industries (Germany both exports and imports cars), which Paul Krugman explained by economies of scale and consumers' taste for variety. Trade has also been sliced into **global value chains**: a smartphone may be designed in California, use chips from Taiwan and South Korea, and be assembled in China or India. The logic is still comparative advantage, applied to tasks rather than to wine and cloth.

## The catch: gains for the country, not for everyone

Comparative advantage proves that trade makes a country as a whole better off: the winners gain more than the losers lose, so in principle the winners could compensate the losers and everyone would come out ahead. The words "in principle" are doing a lot of work.

When England specialises in cloth, its winemakers have to find other jobs. In Ricardo's model they glide effortlessly into the cloth mills. In reality, a laid-off worker in one town may not have the skills, the savings or the willingness to move that a job elsewhere demands.

Economists have long had a theory of who loses. The **Stolper-Samuelson theorem** (1941) says that opening to trade tends to raise the returns to the factor of production a country has in abundance and lower the returns to the factor it has in short supply. For rich countries, which have lots of capital and skilled labour relative to the rest of the world, that suggests less-skilled workers in import-competing industries may lose out.

## The China shock

For a long time many economists assumed these losses were modest and short-lived. Research published from 2013 onwards by David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson changed the conversation.

They studied what happened to American local labour markets after China's rapid integration into the world economy, which accelerated when China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001. Places that specialised in the goods China began to export, such as furniture, textiles, toys and electronics, saw sharp falls in manufacturing employment. A related study with Daron Acemoglu and Brendan Price estimated that rising Chinese import competition cost roughly a million US manufacturing jobs, and perhaps two million jobs in all, between 1999 and 2011.

The striking finding was not the total, which is small relative to an economy of more than 150 million workers. It was how long the damage lasted. Affected areas saw persistently lower wages, lower employment and higher use of disability benefits for a decade or more. People did not simply move to booming regions or retrain. Later work linked the hardest-hit areas to shifts in voting patterns.

Studies in Europe found smaller effects; in Germany, new export markets in eastern Europe cushioned the blow. Meanwhile, consumers everywhere gained from cheaper goods, but those gains were spread thinly, which makes them easy to overlook.

The China shock did not overturn comparative advantage. It reminded economists that the theory says nothing about how quickly or fairly the gains are shared, and that adjustment can be slow, local and painful.

## Tariffs and who really pays

If trade has losers, why not protect them with a **tariff**, a tax on imports? Economists' standard answer is that tariffs usually cost more than they save, and much of the cost falls on the country imposing them.

The question of who bears a tax is called its **incidence**. A tariff is formally paid by the importing firm when goods cross the border. But the firm may pass the cost on to its customers in higher prices, or the foreign exporter may cut its price to stay competitive. Studies of the US tariffs imposed on China and others in 2018 and 2019 found that almost all of the cost was passed through to American importers and consumers, with foreign exporters absorbing little.

Tariffs also have knock-on effects:

- **Higher costs for domestic producers** who use imported parts, which can cost jobs in downstream industries.
- **Retaliation**, as trading partners impose their own tariffs on your exports. Farmers were the main target of Chinese retaliation in 2018.
- **Reduced pressure to improve**, because protected firms face less competition.

The most notorious example is the US Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which raised duties on thousands of goods and prompted retaliation as the Great Depression deepened, becoming a byword for protectionism gone wrong. Since 2025, the United States has again raised tariffs sharply, taking its average rate to levels not seen for many decades, so the question of incidence has become a live one once more.

The strongest case for tariffs is rarely about pure economics. It is about national security (not wanting to depend on a rival for medicines, chips or energy), about protecting young industries until they can stand on their own, or about using the threat of tariffs as a bargaining chip. These arguments are not foolish, but they involve trade-offs that need weighing honestly.

## Where the theory's assumptions break

Ricardo's model is elegant because it is simple, and its simplicity rests on assumptions that do not always hold.

1. **Full employment.** The model assumes displaced workers find new jobs. If they don't, the country loses output rather than reallocating it.
2. **Costless movement between industries.** Skills, homes and families are not as mobile as men moving from vineyards to looms.
3. **Fixed comparative advantage.** In practice, comparative advantage can be built. South Korea and Taiwan turned themselves from exporters of simple goods into world leaders in semiconductors, partly through deliberate industrial policy. That suggests a country's long-term pattern of specialisation is not just a gift of nature.
4. **No externalities.** If an industry generates knowledge that spills over to others, or if losing a whole manufacturing ecosystem makes it hard to rebuild, market prices may not capture the full value of keeping it.
5. **Balanced trade and stable currencies.** Persistent trade imbalances, sometimes sustained by government policy, can make adjustment harder than the textbook allows.
6. **Security and resilience.** The pandemic and the energy shock that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 showed that the cheapest supply chain is not always the safest one.

None of this says trade is bad. It says the gains are real but conditional.

## So what is the case for trade?

The mainstream view is that trade lets countries specialise, reaping Ricardo's gains along with economies of scale, wider choice and the spread of ideas. The integration of China, India and others into world trade coincided with one of the largest falls in extreme poverty in history.

The strongest case against unrestricted trade is not that these gains are imaginary but that they are unevenly shared, that adjustment can scar communities for a generation, and that dependence on rivals can be dangerous. Many economists now accept that trade policy needs pairing with serious support for the people and places that lose out: retraining, help with moving, regional investment and a safety net. Where that support has been thin, the backlash has been strongest.

## Key takeaways

- Comparative advantage means specialising in what you do *relatively* best, measured by opportunity cost. Even a country that is worse at everything can gain from trade.
- Ricardo's wine-and-cloth example shows that specialisation raises total output from the same resources, leaving gains for both sides.
- Trade makes a country better off overall, but not everyone in it. The China shock research showed losses can be concentrated and long-lasting.
- Tariffs are largely paid by the importing country's own firms and consumers, and invite retaliation, though security and strategic arguments can still justify them.
- The theory's assumptions, such as full employment and easy movement between jobs, often fail in practice, which is why the politics of trade is about managing adjustment as much as about the gains themselves.
