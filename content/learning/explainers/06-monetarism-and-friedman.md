In the late 1970s, inflation seemed unstoppable. In Britain, prices had risen by nearly 27% in the year to August 1975. In the US, inflation was heading towards 15% by early 1980. Governments had tried price controls, wage freezes, deals with trade unions and exhortations to restraint. None of it worked for long.

A short, combative economist from the University of Chicago had been saying for years that they were looking in the wrong place. Inflation, Milton Friedman insisted, was not caused by greedy unions, oil sheikhs or profiteering companies. It was caused by governments and central banks creating too much money. Control the money, and you control inflation. That idea, known as **monetarism**, briefly became the governing philosophy on both sides of the Atlantic. Its practical career was short and bumpy. Its intellectual legacy is everywhere.

## The quantity theory of money

Monetarism is a modern version of an old idea: the **quantity theory of money**, which goes back at least to the Scottish philosopher David Hume in the eighteenth century. The American economist Irving Fisher gave it a famous algebraic form in 1911, usually written as:

> MV = PY: the quantity of money (M) times the speed at which it circulates (V, for velocity) equals the price level (P) times real output (Y).

The equation itself is an identity, true by definition. If £100 changes hands five times in a year, it supports £500 of spending, and that spending must equal the value of what is bought. The economics comes in when you make assumptions about the parts.

Monetarists argued that velocity is reasonably stable or at least predictable, and that, in the long run, real output is determined by things like population, skills and technology, not by money. If both V and Y are largely independent of money, then increasing M faster than output grows must, eventually, push up P. Too much money chasing too few goods.

## Friedman's case

Friedman, who won the Nobel prize in economics in 1976, built the case in several stages.

### Money caused the Great Depression

In 1963, Friedman and Anna Schwartz published *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960*. Its most influential chapter argued that the Great Depression was not proof that capitalism was inherently unstable, as many Keynesians believed. Instead, it was largely the result of a central bank failure. Between 1929 and 1933, the US money stock fell by about a third as thousands of banks collapsed, and the Federal Reserve did little to stop it.

The argument has been enormously influential. In 2002, at a conference marking Friedman's 90th birthday, Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor and later its chair, addressed Friedman and Schwartz directly: "Regarding the Great Depression. You're right, we did it. We're very sorry." When the 2008 crisis hit, Bernanke's Fed acted aggressively to stop the money supply and banking system collapsing, partly with that lesson in mind.

### Inflation is a monetary phenomenon

Friedman's most famous line, from 1963, is that "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon". He did not mean that nothing else ever affects prices. A harvest failure can raise food prices; an oil shock can raise energy prices. But such shocks change relative prices, or cause a one-off jump in the price level. A sustained, continuing rise in all prices, he argued, cannot happen unless the money supply keeps growing faster than output. Without more money, if people spend more on petrol they must spend less on something else.

### The natural rate of unemployment

Friedman's most important theoretical contribution came in his presidential address to the American Economic Association in 1967, published the following year. The economist Edmund Phelps reached similar conclusions independently.

At the time, many economists believed in a stable **Phillips curve**: a trade-off in which governments could buy permanently lower unemployment by accepting somewhat higher inflation. Friedman argued that this was an illusion. Any trade-off was temporary. If the government stimulated the economy to push unemployment down, workers would initially be fooled by rising prices. But they would soon come to expect higher inflation and demand higher wages to compensate. Unemployment would drift back to its **natural rate**, set by the structure of the labour market, but now with higher inflation. To keep unemployment below the natural rate, the government would have to keep inflation accelerating.

This **expectations-augmented Phillips curve** was a remarkable prediction, made before the 1970s proved it right. When stagflation arrived, with high inflation and high unemployment together, Friedman's framework explained what the simple Keynesian trade-off could not.

### A rule, not discretion

Friedman's policy prescription followed. Central banks should stop trying to fine-tune the economy, because monetary policy works with "long and variable lags" that make precise steering impossible. Instead, they should let the money supply grow at a steady, pre-announced rate, roughly in line with the economy's long-run growth. This became known as the **k-percent rule**.

## Monetarism in practice

### Volcker's shock

In August 1979, President Jimmy Carter appointed Paul Volcker as chair of the Federal Reserve. In October that year, Volcker announced that the Fed would target the growth of bank reserves and the money supply, letting interest rates go wherever was needed. Rates went very high indeed. The federal funds rate peaked at around 20% in 1981. The US suffered a deep recession in 1981-82, with unemployment peaking at about 10.8% in late 1982.

But inflation broke. From around 14% in 1980, US inflation fell to around 3-4% by 1983. Whether this was a triumph of monetarism or of a central bank simply willing to raise interest rates high enough is debated. The Fed's money targets were frequently missed, and by 1982 it had quietly moved away from strict money targeting.

### Thatcher's experiment

In Britain, Margaret Thatcher's government, elected in 1979, embraced monetarism explicitly. Its 1980 **Medium-Term Financial Strategy** set declining targets for a measure of broad money called sterling M3, alongside plans to reduce government borrowing.

The targets were repeatedly overshot. Yet monetary conditions were in practice very tight: interest rates were high and the pound soared, partly thanks to North Sea oil. Manufacturing was hit hard. Unemployment rose above three million in the early 1980s. In March 1981, 364 economists signed a letter to *The Times* arguing that the government's policies had no basis in economic theory and would deepen the recession. The government persisted, and the economy began recovering later that year, which the policy's defenders cite as vindication.

Inflation fell from around 18% in 1980 to under 5% by 1983. As in the US, there is still a lively argument about how much credit belongs to monetarism specifically, as opposed to a severe recession and high interest rates.

## Why money targeting was abandoned

By the late 1980s, almost every central bank that had tried to target the money supply had given up. The core problem was that the relationship between money and spending, which monetarists had assumed was stable, broke down.

Financial deregulation and innovation, from interest-bearing current accounts to credit cards, changed how people held money. Velocity became unpredictable. The money aggregates that central banks targeted stopped tracking inflation reliably. This illustrated what became known as **Goodhart's law**, after the economist Charles Goodhart: once a statistical relationship is used as a target for policy, it tends to break down.

The Governor of the Bank of Canada at the time, Gerald Bouey, summed it up memorably: "We didn't abandon the monetary aggregates, they abandoned us."

Through the 1990s, central banks shifted to targeting inflation directly, using interest rates as their main tool. New Zealand led the way around 1990; the UK followed in 1992. The ECB kept a "monetary pillar" in its strategy for many years, a lingering nod to the importance of money.

## A modern echo: 2020-23

Monetarism enjoyed an unexpected moment in the spotlight after the pandemic. In 2020 and 2021, the broad US money supply (M2) grew at its fastest pace in decades, peaking at more than 25% year-on-year in early 2021, as governments spent heavily and central banks bought bonds. Some economists with monetarist leanings warned that high inflation would follow. It did.

Critics point out that the link was not tidy: mainstream explanations for the 2021-23 inflation also emphasised supply chain disruptions, energy prices and overheated demand, and M2 then fell in 2023 without inflation collapsing in step. The episode did, though, persuade many economists that monetary aggregates had been too readily ignored.

## The legacy

Even though strict monetarism failed in practice, much of Friedman's thinking became part of mainstream economics.

- **Inflation is the central bank's responsibility.** The idea that monetary policy, rather than incomes policies or price controls, should control inflation is now standard.
- **Expectations matter.** The natural rate and the role of expectations sit at the heart of modern macroeconomics.
- **There is no permanent trade-off.** Few economists now believe that governments can buy lower unemployment indefinitely with higher inflation.
- **Rules and credibility.** The emphasis on predictable policy fed into the case for independent central banks with clear targets.
- **Preventing banking collapses.** The lesson of the Depression shaped the response to 2008 and 2020.

Critics, from Keynesians to post-Keynesians, argue that monetarism underplayed the importance of demand, that the natural rate cannot be measured with any precision, and that the human costs of the early 1980s recessions were far higher than they needed to be. Supporters reply that the Great Inflation of the 1970s had to be stopped, and that the credibility won then underpinned decades of low inflation.

## Key takeaways

- Monetarism holds that sustained inflation is caused by money growing faster than output, building on the old quantity theory of money.
- Friedman and Schwartz blamed the Great Depression on a collapse in the money supply that the Fed failed to prevent, a lesson that shaped later crisis responses.
- Friedman's natural rate theory predicted stagflation and undermined the idea of a permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
- Volcker and Thatcher-era experiments brought inflation down at high cost, but money targets proved unreliable and were abandoned as velocity became unstable.
- Today's inflation-targeting central banks owe much to Friedman, even though they steer with interest rates rather than the money supply.
