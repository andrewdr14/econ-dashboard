Once a month, a single number is released that can move currency markets, decide whether millions of mortgage payments go up, and set the size of pension and benefit increases. In the UK it arrives from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) at 7am; in the US it comes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics at 8.30 in the morning, Washington time. "Inflation rose to 3.2%," says the headline. But what, exactly, rose by 3.2%? Whose prices, measured how, and compared with what?

Measuring inflation means turning the chaotic, ever-changing world of things people buy into one tidy percentage. Every step involves choices, and different countries have made different ones. Understanding them is the best way to read inflation news without being misled.

## The basket: an imaginary shopping trolley

Statisticians begin with an idea called the **basket of goods and services**. Imagine a giant shopping trolley containing a little of everything a typical household spends money on: bread, petrol, rent, haircuts, cinema tickets, broadband, train fares, car insurance and so on. The UK basket contains roughly 700 representative items, and the ONS collects many thousands of individual prices each month, some by staff visiting shops, increasingly from scanner and web data.

Each item is then given a **weight** reflecting how much of the typical household budget goes on it. If households spend ten times as much on electricity as on tea, a 10% rise in electricity prices matters ten times as much to the index. The weights come from large household spending surveys and the national accounts, and they are updated every year.

The basket itself is also refreshed annually, which produces a small news story each spring as items come and go. Air fryers, for example, were added to the UK basket in 2024. The aim is not to price a fixed list forever, but to keep tracking what people actually buy.

Put the weighted prices together and you get a **price index**: a number set to 100 in a reference year. If the index is 133 today, the basket costs a third more than in the reference year. **Inflation** is simply the percentage change in that index, usually compared with the same month a year earlier.

## Why there is more than one measure

If the idea is so simple, why do newspapers juggle so many acronyms? Because there are genuinely different questions you might want answered, and some disputed technical choices about the answers.

### The UK: CPI, CPIH and RPI

The UK publishes three main measures.

- **CPI (Consumer Prices Index)** is the measure the Bank of England targets, at 2%. It follows internationally agreed rules, which makes it comparable with the rest of Europe.
- **CPIH** is CPI plus **owner occupiers' housing costs** and council tax. Homeowners do not pay rent, but living in a house you own still has a cost. The ONS estimates it by asking what owners would pay to rent an equivalent home, a method called **rental equivalence**. Since 2017, CPIH has been the ONS's lead measure, although CPI still drives monetary policy.
- **RPI (Retail Prices Index)** is the oldest, dating from the 1940s. It includes mortgage interest payments and house-price-linked depreciation, and it uses an averaging formula that statisticians agree tends to push it upwards. It lost its status as a National Statistic in 2013. Yet it survives because contracts, index-linked government bonds, some rail fares and student loan interest have historically been tied to it. The government and UK Statistics Authority have announced that RPI's methods will be brought into line with CPIH from 2030.

The gap matters. RPI has typically run around a percentage point above CPI, so a pay deal or a price rise linked to RPI is noticeably more generous (or costly) than one linked to CPI.

### The US: CPI and PCE

The US has its own **CPI**, which gets most of the headlines. But the Federal Reserve formally targets a different measure: the **Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index**, produced by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. The Fed adopted an explicit 2% PCE target in 2012.

PCE differs in three main ways. It covers a wider range of spending, including things paid for on households' behalf, such as employer-provided health insurance. Its weights come from business data rather than household surveys, so housing gets a smaller share. And its formula lets the weights shift as people substitute cheaper goods for dearer ones, such as switching from beef to chicken when beef prices jump. The upshot is that PCE inflation has tended to run a few tenths of a percentage point below CPI inflation.

### The euro area: HICP

The euro area uses the **Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP)**, compiled by Eurostat from national data on common rules so that inflation in Portugal and Finland can be compared and combined. The European Central Bank (ECB) targets 2% HICP inflation over the medium term. HICP currently leaves out owner-occupied housing costs, a long-debated gap; the ECB has said it would like them included eventually.

## Headline and core

The all-items figure is called **headline inflation**. It is what households experience. But it is noisy, because a few categories, especially energy and food, can swing wildly for reasons that have little to do with the underlying state of the economy: a war, a poor harvest, a cold winter.

So statisticians also publish **core inflation**, which strips out the most volatile items. In the US, core usually means excluding food and energy. In the UK and euro area, core typically excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco.

Core is not "the inflation that really matters". People still have to buy food and heat their homes. Its value is as a guide to where inflation is heading: if core is stable while headline jumps because of an oil price spike, the jump may fade on its own. If core is climbing, price rises are spreading across the economy and are likelier to stick.

Central banks look at other underlying measures too. Some research institutions publish "trimmed mean" and "median" inflation, which each month discard the items with the most extreme price changes, whatever they happen to be. Many central banks watch **services inflation** closely, because services prices are heavily driven by wages and so reflect domestic pressures rather than global commodity markets.

## Year-on-year, month-on-month and base effects

Headline inflation figures are almost always **year-on-year (y/y)**: prices this month compared with the same month last year. This neatly sidesteps seasonal patterns, such as clothing sales in January, but it has a drawback. A y/y rate is a twelve-month average of history. It tells you what has happened, not necessarily what is happening now.

**Month-on-month (m/m)** changes are more up to date, but they are small and jumpy. Analysts often convert them to an annualised rate, asking what the annual rate would be if this month's pace continued for a year. A rise of 0.2% in a month annualises to about 2.4%; a rise of 0.5% annualises to about 6.2%. Annualised three-month or six-month rates are a popular compromise between timeliness and noise.

The y/y method also creates **base effects**. Because each month's figure is compared with the same month a year earlier, what happened back then matters as much as what is happening now. Suppose energy prices leap in one particular month. For the next eleven months, that jump stays inside the twelve-month comparison and keeps inflation high. In the twelfth month, the jump drops out of the comparison, and inflation can fall sharply even if nothing at all happens to prices that month.

## A real-world episode: the 2022-23 surge and fall

The inflation spike after the pandemic and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine illustrates all of this. UK CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% in October 2022, the highest in about four decades. Euro area HICP inflation peaked at 10.6% the same month, and US CPI inflation reached 9.1% in June 2022.

Much of the initial surge was energy and food, so headline rose well above core. Then base effects worked in reverse. As the giant energy price rises of 2022 dropped out of the twelve-month comparison during 2023, headline inflation fell steeply. In the UK, for example, the October 2022 household energy jump fell out of the figures in October 2023, and CPI inflation dropped from 6.7% to 4.6% in a single month.

Yet central banks did not declare victory. Core inflation and services inflation fell more slowly, because wage growth was strong and price rises had spread throughout the economy. That is precisely the pattern that the distinction between headline and core is designed to reveal.

## Why central banks target 2%

Why 2% rather than zero? Stable prices sound like the obvious goal. There are several reasons the number ended up slightly positive.

1. **Measurement bias.** Price indices tend to overstate true inflation a little, because they struggle to capture quality improvements and new products. A US review in 1996, the Boskin Commission, estimated the US CPI overstated inflation by about a percentage point a year at the time. Measured inflation of zero might mean real prices were falling.
2. **A buffer against deflation.** Falling prices can be dangerous: people delay purchases, and the real burden of debts grows. A small positive rate gives a margin of safety.
3. **Room to cut interest rates.** Interest rates usually sit above inflation. With 2% inflation, normal interest rates are higher, leaving central banks more room to cut in a downturn before hitting zero.
4. **Greasing the wheels.** Employers rarely cut wages in cash terms. Modest inflation allows real wages to adjust in struggling sectors without nominal pay cuts.

The specific figure owes something to history. New Zealand pioneered inflation targeting around 1990, and as the idea spread through the 1990s, around 2% became the international norm. The Bank of England received its 2% CPI target in 2003, having previously targeted 2.5% on a variant of RPI. Some economists argue a higher target, perhaps 3% or 4%, would give more room to cut rates; others warn that changing the target would damage credibility. For now, 2% remains the near-universal standard among advanced economies.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Inflation falling means prices are falling."** No. Falling inflation means prices are rising more slowly. Prices only fall when inflation is negative, which is called deflation. After the 2022 surge, price levels stayed permanently higher even once inflation returned towards 2%.
- **"My inflation is different, so the figure is wrong."** Both can be true. The index reflects an average household. Renters, drivers, pensioners and families with young children all have different baskets. The ONS publishes household-specific measures for precisely this reason.
- **"Core inflation ignores food and fuel, so it is meaningless."** It is a forecasting tool, not a cost-of-living measure. Central banks look at both.

## Key takeaways

- Inflation measures the change in the cost of a weighted basket of goods and services, refreshed every year to track what people actually buy.
- Different measures answer different questions: the Bank of England targets CPI, the Fed targets PCE and the ECB targets HICP, all at 2%. The UK's RPI is a legacy measure that tends to run higher.
- Headline inflation is what households feel; core inflation strips out volatile items to show underlying trends.
- Year-on-year rates are affected by base effects, so inflation can drop or jump simply because of what happened twelve months earlier.
- Central banks aim for 2% rather than zero to allow for measurement bias, keep a buffer against deflation and leave room to cut interest rates.
