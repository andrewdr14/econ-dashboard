In 1964 the writer Donald Horne called Australia "the lucky country". He meant it as an insult: a nation that prospered on its natural resources and its British inheritance while its leaders showed little imagination. The phrase stuck, but its meaning softened into a boast. Australia has some of the richest mineral deposits on earth, sits on the doorstep of the fastest-growing region in the world, and from 1991 until the pandemic went nearly 29 years without a recession, a record for a rich country. Yet luck is not a strategy. With one customer, China, buying around a third of its exports and housing among the least affordable in the developed world, Australians increasingly ask how much of their success was skill and how much was simply what lay under the ground.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 27 million, growing quickly thanks to immigration, and highly urbanised around five big coastal cities.
- **Economy:** roughly $1.8 trillion in 2024 (about A$2.7 trillion); around $65,000 per person.
- **Biggest exports:** iron ore, coal, liquefied natural gas (LNG), gold, education services, beef and wheat.
- **Currency:** the Australian dollar, freely floating since 1983 and widely traded as a "proxy" for Asian growth and commodity prices.
- **System:** a federal parliamentary democracy and constitutional monarchy of six states and two territories.

## How the economy makes its money

It may surprise visitors, but most Australians do not work anywhere near a mine. Like other rich countries, Australia is predominantly a services economy: health care, education, finance, retail, construction and professional services employ the large majority of workers. Mining directly employs only around 2% of the workforce.

But mining punches far above its weight in output, profits, exports and tax. In good years it accounts for well over a tenth of GDP and the majority of goods exports. The heart of it is the Pilbara in Western Australia, where enormous open-cut mines owned by BHP, Rio Tinto, Fortescue and others feed iron ore by rail to ports such as Port Hedland and on to Chinese steel mills. Queensland and New South Wales produce coal, both metallurgical coal for steelmaking and thermal coal for power stations. Offshore gas fields off Western Australia and the Northern Territory, and coal-seam gas in Queensland, made Australia one of the world's biggest LNG exporters.

Resources and energy exports have been worth roughly A$400 billion a year in recent years, peaking at more than A$450 billion in 2022-23 when energy prices spiked after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. When commodity prices rise, Australia's "terms of trade" (the price of its exports relative to its imports) improve, profits and company taxes surge, and the federal budget benefits. When they fall, the reverse happens.

### The other exports

Two less obvious earners matter a lot. **International education** is one of Australia's largest export industries: foreign students paying fees and living costs, mostly from China and India, bring in tens of billions of dollars a year. **Agriculture** is efficient and export-oriented, with Australia a major exporter of beef, wheat, barley, wool and wine. Tourism adds more.

### Superannuation

Australia also has one of the world's biggest pension systems. Since 1992 employers have been required to pay a share of wages into workers' private retirement accounts, known as superannuation or "super". The rate rose to 12% of wages in July 2025. The total pool now exceeds A$4 trillion, larger than the whole economy, making Australian super funds powerful investors at home and abroad, including in infrastructure.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Australia's export basket is dominated by raw materials, its import basket by manufactured goods: cars, machinery, electronics, fuel and medicines. Since the early 2000s Asia, and above all China, has been the centre of gravity.

| Export destination | Approx. share of goods and services exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| China | ~32% |
| Japan | ~10% |
| South Korea | ~6% |
| India | ~5% |
| United States | ~5% |

Iron ore alone is typically worth more than A$100 billion a year, and China buys the large majority of it. On the import side, China is also the largest supplier (around a quarter), followed by the United States and the countries of South-East Asia. Australia has free-trade agreements with all its major partners, including China (2015), Japan, Korea, the UK (2023) and India (an interim deal in 2022).

### The China dispute of 2020-23

The risks of dependence were laid bare in 2020. After Canberra called for an independent inquiry into the origins of COVID-19, and amid a wider deterioration in relations (over foreign interference laws and the exclusion of Huawei from 5G networks), Beijing hit back. It imposed tariffs of around 80% on Australian barley and up to about 218% on wine, and informally blocked coal, lobster, timber, cotton and some beef.

The campaign hurt particular industries, especially winemakers, who lost what had been their biggest market. But it largely failed as economic coercion. Iron ore, which China could not easily replace, was untouched, and its price soared. Coal and barley found new buyers in India and elsewhere. After the change of government in Canberra in 2022 relations thawed, and between 2023 and late 2024 the restrictions were lifted one by one. The episode is now studied worldwide as a case of how diversified commodity exporters can withstand pressure.

## Strengths

- **World-class resources.** Australia is the largest producer of iron ore and lithium, a top exporter of coal, LNG and gold, and holds significant reserves of rare earths, cobalt and other critical minerals.
- **Strong institutions.** An independent central bank (the Reserve Bank of Australia), a floating currency, stable politics and the rule of law. The government holds top-tier credit ratings.
- **Skilled immigration.** Around 30% of residents were born overseas. A points-based system draws in young, skilled workers, keeping the population younger than in most rich countries.
- **Flexibility.** The floating dollar acts as a shock absorber: when commodity prices fall, the currency usually does too, cushioning exporters.
- **Location.** Proximity to Asia's growing markets, especially India and South-East Asia, offers future opportunities.

## Vulnerabilities

**Concentration.** Australia's exports are concentrated in a handful of commodities sold largely to one country. A sharp slowdown in Chinese steel production, as China's property sector shrinks, is the single biggest economic risk.

**Housing.** Australian home prices are among the highest in the world relative to incomes, particularly in Sydney and Melbourne. Causes include strong population growth, restrictive planning rules, slow construction, tax breaks for investors (*negative gearing*, which lets landlords deduct rental losses against other income, and a capital gains tax discount) and decades of falling interest rates. Household debt is among the highest in the developed world, which makes consumers sensitive to interest rate rises.

**Productivity.** Productivity growth has been weak for over a decade, and the economy has relied on population growth for much of its expansion; per-person income growth has been sluggish.

**Climate and energy.** Australia is highly exposed to droughts, bushfires and floods, and its export earnings rely on fossil fuels the world is trying to phase out. Ageing coal power stations are closing, and the switch to renewables at home has brought grid and price challenges.

**Few manufacturers.** Car manufacturing ended in 2017. Australia exports raw ore and imports finished goods, including the batteries made from its own lithium.

## Politics and power

Australia's federal parliament has two houses: the House of Representatives, where government is formed, and a powerful Senate elected by proportional representation. Voting is compulsory, and turnout is usually around 90%. The British monarch, **King Charles III**, is head of state, represented by the Governor-General; a 1999 referendum on becoming a republic was defeated.

As of mid-2026, **Anthony Albanese** of the Labor Party is prime minister. First elected in 2022, he won a second term in May 2025 with a substantially increased majority, while the opposition Liberal-National Coalition suffered one of its worst results. Jim Chalmers is treasurer. The government's priorities include housing supply (a target of 1.2 million new homes over five years), the energy transition and a "Future Made in Australia" industrial policy offering incentives for green metals, hydrogen and critical minerals processing.

Foreign policy balances economics and security. The United States is Australia's chief security ally, cemented by the **AUKUS** pact (2021) under which Australia is to acquire nuclear-powered submarines, while China is its biggest trading partner. In late 2025 Canberra and Washington signed a framework on critical minerals, aimed at building supply chains outside China.

## The history that shaped it

Indigenous Australians have lived on the continent for at least 50,000 years. British colonisation from 1788 dispossessed them, with lasting consequences that remain a central question in Australian politics; a 2023 referendum to create an Indigenous advisory "Voice" to parliament was rejected.

The colonies grew rich on wool and then gold: the gold rushes of the 1850s brought a huge wave of immigrants. After Federation in 1901, Australia built what historians call the "Australian Settlement": high tariffs to protect manufacturing, centralised wage-fixing and the racist White Australia immigration policy, dismantled from the late 1960s and formally ended in 1973. The Britain-centred economy reoriented towards Asia after the UK joined the European Community in 1973, with Japan becoming the leading customer.

The great turning point came in the 1980s. The Labor governments of Bob Hawke and Paul Keating floated the dollar in 1983, deregulated banking, cut tariffs drastically, introduced compulsory superannuation and made the central bank independent in practice. Many economists credit these reforms, plus the GST introduced by John Howard's government in 2000, for the long run of growth that followed the early-1990s recession.

Then came the **mining boom**. From around 2003, Chinese demand pushed iron ore and coal prices to unprecedented levels, triggering a vast investment wave that peaked in 2012. A proposed "super profits" tax on miners in 2010 contributed to the downfall of Prime Minister Kevin Rudd, a reminder of the industry's political weight. Australia came through the 2008 global financial crisis without a recession, and its record run ended only with the pandemic in 2020.

## What to watch

- **China's steel output.** The biggest swing factor for iron ore prices and for the federal budget.
- **Critical minerals.** Whether Australia can move beyond digging up lithium and rare earths into processing and refining.
- **Housing supply.** Whether construction catches up with population growth.
- **Interest rates.** The RBA began cutting rates in 2025; how far it goes matters for mortgage holders.
- **Migration.** Net overseas migration hit a record of over half a million in 2022-23 and became a political flashpoint; the government has sought to bring it down.

## Key takeaways

- Australia is a services economy at home but a mining economy in its exports, with iron ore, coal and LNG the biggest earners.
- China buys around a third of its exports; Beijing's 2020-23 trade restrictions hurt some industries but failed to bend Canberra.
- Market reforms in the 1980s and the China-driven mining boom underpinned nearly three decades without recession.
- Housing affordability, weak productivity and reliance on a few commodities are its main vulnerabilities.
- As of mid-2026 Anthony Albanese's Labor government is betting on housing supply, critical minerals and green industry to diversify the economy.
