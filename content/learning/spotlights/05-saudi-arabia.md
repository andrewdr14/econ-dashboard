Ten years ago, a Saudi woman could not legally drive a car, cinemas were banned, and almost all Saudis who worked did so for the state. Today women drive, work in shops and offices in growing numbers, concerts and football draw huge crowds, and cranes rise over the Riyadh skyline. The transformation is driven by a plan called Vision 2030 and by the man behind it, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. Its premise is simple and urgent: Saudi Arabia's prosperity has rested on oil for eighty years, and the world will eventually need less of it. Whether the kingdom can build a broad, private-sector economy before that happens is one of the great economic experiments of our time.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 35 million, of whom around 40% are foreign residents.
- **Economy:** about $1.1 trillion of output in 2024, the largest in the Middle East.
- **Oil:** among the world's two or three largest producers, typically pumping around 9-10 million barrels a day, with the largest spare capacity of any country.
- **Currency:** the riyal, pegged at 3.75 to the US dollar since 1986.
- **Sovereign wealth:** the Public Investment Fund (PIF) manages assets reported at over $900 billion.
- **Signature companies:** Saudi Aramco, SABIC, Ma'aden and the Saudi National Bank.

## How the economy makes its money

Oil is the centre of everything. Saudi Arabia holds some of the world's largest proven reserves, and its fields, such as Ghawar, the largest conventional oilfield ever discovered, are among the cheapest to operate anywhere. Production and export are run by **Saudi Aramco**, the state oil company, which is one of the most profitable companies in the world.

Oil's dominance shows up in three places. First, petroleum and related products account for roughly three-quarters of goods exports. Second, oil revenues typically provide around 60% of government income, mostly via Aramco's royalties, taxes and dividends. Third, much of the rest of the economy, from construction to retail, is ultimately powered by government spending financed by oil.

Officially, oil activities now account for less than half of GDP, and the government highlights that "non-oil activities" have grown to around half of the economy. But that figure includes large amounts of government services and activity funded by oil money, so the economy remains more dependent on hydrocarbons than the headline share suggests.

Beyond crude oil, Saudi Arabia has built a large **petrochemicals** industry, led by SABIC (now majority-owned by Aramco), which turns cheap feedstock into plastics and fertilisers. **Mining** is a newer priority, with the state miner Ma'aden developing phosphate, gold and aluminium. Financial services, retail and construction are large, and **tourism and entertainment** are growing fast from a low base: the kingdom began issuing tourist visas only in 2019, and religious tourism to Mecca and Medina remains a major draw.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Saudi Arabia runs a large surplus in goods when oil prices are high, but this can shrink quickly when they fall. It runs a deficit in services and sends large remittances abroad from its foreign workers.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Crude oil | ~60% |
| Refined petroleum products | ~15% |
| Plastics and petrochemicals | ~10% |
| Other chemicals and fertilisers | ~4% |
| Other goods (incl. re-exports) | ~11% |

Most of the oil goes east. **China** is the largest buyer, followed by Japan, India and South Korea; Asia together takes the large majority of Saudi crude. Imports, mainly machinery, vehicles, electronics, food and pharmaceuticals, come chiefly from China, the United States, the United Arab Emirates, India and Europe. The United States remains the kingdom's most important security partner and a major supplier of arms and technology, even though it now buys relatively little Saudi oil.

## Strengths

- **Cheap oil.** Saudi crude is among the cheapest in the world to produce, meaning the kingdom can remain profitable even when prices fall to levels that hurt rivals.
- **Spare capacity and market power.** The ability to quickly raise or cut output makes Saudi Arabia the de facto leader of OPEC+.
- **Financial firepower.** Large foreign assets at the central bank and the PIF, combined with moderate public debt of roughly 30% of GDP, give the state room to borrow and invest.
- **A young population.** Around two-thirds of Saudi citizens are under 35, a potential source of workers and consumers.
- **Strategic location.** The kingdom sits between Europe, Africa and Asia, with coastlines on the Red Sea and the Gulf.

## Vulnerabilities

**Oil prices and the fiscal breakeven.** The IMF estimates the oil price Saudi Arabia needs to balance its budget, the **fiscal breakeven**, at around $90 a barrel in recent years, well above the prices that prevailed for much of 2025. The government has run deficits and borrowed heavily at home and on international markets, and spending plans for Vision 2030 have had to be reprioritised.

**The energy transition.** In the long run, falling global demand for oil, from electric vehicles and renewable energy, threatens the core of the economy. The timing is uncertain, but the direction drives the whole diversification strategy.

**Execution risk on giga-projects.** The flagship projects, including **NEOM**, a futuristic region in the north-west featuring "The Line", a linear city planned to stretch 170 kilometres, have been scaled back and delayed as costs mounted. Critics argue that too much capital has gone into prestige projects with uncertain returns.

**Private sector depth.** Many businesses still depend on government contracts. Foreign direct investment has fallen short of official targets.

**Regional security.** The kingdom lies in a volatile neighbourhood. In 2019, drone and missile attacks on Abqaiq temporarily knocked out around half of Saudi oil output. Wars in Yemen, Gaza and tensions with Iran all carry risks.

**Reputation and rights.** Restrictions on political freedoms and the 2018 killing of the journalist Jamal Khashoggi in the Saudi consulate in Istanbul have weighed on the kingdom's image with some Western investors and governments.

## Politics and power

Saudi Arabia is an absolute monarchy with no elected parliament; an appointed Consultative Assembly (Shura Council) advises the king. Legitimacy has traditionally rested on the royal family's alliance with the religious establishment, and on sharing oil wealth through public jobs, free healthcare and education, and subsidised energy.

As of mid-2026, **King Salman**, who acceded in 2015, remains on the throne. Real power lies with his son, **Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman**, often called MBS, who has also been prime minister since 2022. He has concentrated authority to an extent unusual in Saudi history, sidelining rival princes, curbing the influence of religious conservatives, and, in a 2017 anti-corruption drive, detaining hundreds of businessmen and royals at the Ritz-Carlton hotel in Riyadh until they surrendered assets.

His **Vision 2030**, launched in 2016, set targets to raise the private sector's share of the economy, increase non-oil revenue, lift women's employment, and develop tourism, entertainment, mining and manufacturing. Some results have been striking. Women's participation in the labour force roughly doubled, to over 30%. Saudi unemployment has fallen to record lows. Value added tax, introduced in 2018 and tripled to 15% in 2020, now provides a significant non-oil revenue stream.

The engine of much of this is the **Public Investment Fund (PIF)**, chaired by the crown prince. It invests at home, founding companies, building giga-projects and backing sports and entertainment, and abroad, holding stakes in companies ranging from Uber to Nintendo and financing the LIV Golf tour. Riyadh will host the World Expo in 2030 and the FIFA World Cup in 2034.

## The history that shaped it

The modern kingdom was founded in 1932 by Abdulaziz ibn Saud, who united much of the Arabian Peninsula. Just six years later, in 1938, American geologists struck commercial quantities of oil at Dammam. The Arabian American Oil Company (Aramco) developed the fields, and the kingdom gradually took control of it, completing full ownership in 1980.

Saudi Arabia was a founding member of **OPEC** in 1960. In 1973, Arab producers imposed an oil embargo on countries backing Israel in the Yom Kippur War, and prices quadrupled. The windfall transformed the kingdom almost overnight, funding roads, cities, universities and a vast welfare state.

But oil wealth has always been volatile. When prices collapsed in the mid-1980s, the kingdom ran long deficits and accumulated debt. In 1986 it fixed the riyal to the dollar, a **peg** that has held ever since, giving stability to an economy whose main export is priced in dollars, though it means Saudi monetary policy broadly follows the US Federal Reserve.

The 2014-16 oil price crash, when prices fell from over $100 to under $30, was the trigger for Vision 2030. It exposed how quickly the kingdom could burn through its reserves. In 2019, Aramco sold about 1.5% of its shares on the Riyadh stock exchange in one of the largest share sales ever, raising money for the PIF.

Since 2016, Saudi Arabia has managed oil supply through **OPEC+**, an alliance with Russia and other producers. In 2023 it led deep output cuts to support prices; from 2025 the group began unwinding them, choosing to regain market share even at the cost of lower prices.

## What to watch

- **Oil prices and OPEC+ decisions**, which drive revenues, deficits and borrowing.
- **The reprioritisation of Vision 2030**, including which giga-projects are scaled back and how capital is redirected.
- **Saudisation.** Quotas known as *Nitaqat* require firms to employ a share of Saudi nationals; whether these create productive private-sector jobs, rather than box-ticking, is a key test.
- **Foreign investment** and whether international firms commit capital, not just regional headquarters.
- **Regional diplomacy**, including relations with Iran, Israel and the United States.

## Key takeaways

- Oil provides around three-quarters of goods exports and around 60% of government revenue, and Saudi Arabia is the pivotal producer in OPEC+.
- Vision 2030 aims to diversify into tourism, mining, manufacturing and services, with the Public Investment Fund as its main engine.
- Social changes, including women's entry into the workforce, have been rapid and economically significant.
- The fiscal breakeven oil price is high, so lower oil prices force spending cuts or more borrowing.
- The riyal's peg to the dollar and the crown prince's centralised power provide stability, but concentrate risk.
