Drive through the small towns of Baden-Württemberg and you will pass unremarkable industrial estates that hide world leaders: a firm that makes most of the planet's industrial dowels, another that dominates the market for tunnel-boring machines, a third whose pumps sit inside power stations on every continent. This is the quiet engine of the German economy. For decades it produced a simple, enviable formula: make things the world wants, make them better than anyone else, and sell them abroad. The formula made Germany the largest economy in Europe and the third largest in the world. But since about 2018 the economy has barely grown, and the question hanging over Berlin is whether the formula itself has run out of road.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 83 million, the largest in the European Union.
- **Economy:** about €4.3 trillion of output in 2024, around a quarter of the euro area's total.
- **Currency:** the euro, with monetary policy set by the European Central Bank in Frankfurt.
- **Model:** export-led manufacturing; exports of goods and services worth roughly 40% of GDP in 2024.
- **Signature industries:** cars, machinery, chemicals, pharmaceuticals and electrical equipment.
- **Recent performance:** output shrank slightly in both 2023 and 2024 and was close to flat in 2025.

## How the economy makes its money

Like every rich country, Germany is mostly a services economy: retail, healthcare, finance, logistics and public administration account for about two-thirds of output. What makes it unusual is how much it still makes. Manufacturing contributes roughly 18-20% of gross value added, compared with around 10% in the United States, the United Kingdom and France. That share has held up for decades while other rich countries deindustrialised.

The best-known part of that industry is the car sector. Volkswagen, BMW and Mercedes-Benz, along with suppliers such as Bosch, Continental and ZF, employ several hundred thousand people directly and many more indirectly. Behind them stands the chemical giant BASF, whose sprawling site in Ludwigshafen is one of the largest integrated chemical complexes in the world, and Siemens, which spans factory automation, rail and medical technology.

Yet the real backbone is the **Mittelstand**: the tens of thousands of small and medium-sized firms, often family-owned and rooted in one town for generations. Many are "hidden champions", a term coined by the management writer Hermann Simon for companies that are obscure to the public but hold dominant global shares in narrow niches, such as industrial valves, labelling machines or surgical instruments. They tend to think in decades rather than quarters, reinvest profits, and rely on long relationships with local savings banks (*Sparkassen*) and co-operative banks.

Two institutions help explain the quality of what they make. The first is the **dual vocational training system**, in which young people split their time between a company and a vocational school, emerging after about three years with a recognised qualification. It produces the skilled machinists, electricians and technicians that precision manufacturing needs. The second is **co-determination** (*Mitbestimmung*): in large companies, workers elect up to half the supervisory board, and works councils have a formal say over working conditions. Critics argue this slows decision-making; supporters say it builds trust, which helped Germany avoid mass lay-offs in 2009 and 2020 through the *Kurzarbeit* short-time work scheme, where the state tops up wages while firms cut hours rather than jobs.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Germany has run large trade surpluses for most of the past two decades, exporting far more than it imports. The surplus in goods was roughly €240 billion in 2024, and the wider current account surplus has often been 5-8% of GDP, among the largest in the world in absolute terms.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Motor vehicles and parts | ~15% |
| Machinery | ~13% |
| Chemical products | ~9% |
| Pharmaceuticals | ~7% |
| Computers, electronics and optical goods | ~8% |
| Electrical equipment | ~6% |

On the import side, Germany buys energy, raw materials, electronics, clothing and a great many components that feed its own factories. The trade is strongly European: roughly half of exports go to other EU countries, with France, the Netherlands, Poland and Italy among the biggest buyers. Outside Europe, the **United States** became Germany's largest single trading partner again in 2024, taking roughly a tenth of exports, while **China**, which held that title for eight years until then, is the largest source of imports.

The surplus is a point of pride at home and a point of friction abroad. Critics, including successive US administrations and the IMF, argue that persistent surpluses reflect too little domestic spending and investment, and that they push deficits onto trading partners. Defenders reply that the surplus reflects competitive products and an ageing society saving for retirement.

## Strengths

- **Engineering depth.** Few countries can match Germany's ecosystem of suppliers, skilled workers and applied research, notably through the network of Fraunhofer institutes that turn research into industrial applications.
- **Low public debt.** General government debt was roughly 63% of GDP in 2024, low by the standards of its peers, giving it room to borrow if it chooses.
- **Social partnership.** Relations between employers and unions are generally co-operative, and strikes are less frequent than in France or the UK.
- **A diversified geography.** Unlike countries where everything happens in the capital, German economic activity is spread across Munich, Stuttgart, Frankfurt, Hamburg, the Rhine-Ruhr and beyond.
- **The single market.** Membership of the EU and the euro gives German firms tariff-free access to some 450 million consumers and removes currency risk on much of their trade.

## Vulnerabilities

**Energy.** For years, German industry ran on cheap Russian natural gas piped across the Baltic. In 2021 Russia supplied more than half of Germany's gas. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, those flows collapsed and wholesale prices spiked to many times their usual level. The country adapted faster than many expected, building floating liquefied natural gas terminals in months and cutting consumption. But energy remains markedly more expensive than in the US or China, and energy-intensive industries such as chemicals, glass and steel have cut output, with some firms shifting investment abroad. The decision to close the last nuclear plants in April 2023 remains contested.

**Cars and China.** For two decades, China was the German car industry's best customer; at their peak, Volkswagen sold close to 40% of its cars there. Now Chinese manufacturers such as BYD lead in affordable electric vehicles and battery technology, and German brands are losing share in China while facing new competition in Europe. The shift from the combustion engine, where German firms excelled, to electric drivetrains, where software and batteries matter most, threatens a large network of suppliers.

**Trade exposure.** An economy that exports around 40% of its output is vulnerable to tariffs and trade wars. US tariffs announced in 2025, and the EU-US arrangement that set a 15% tariff on most EU goods, hit German exporters directly.

**Demographics and labour shortages.** Germany's population is ageing quickly, and the baby-boomer generation is retiring. Without immigration, the working-age population would shrink sharply in the coming decade. Firms already report shortages of skilled workers, from nurses to IT specialists.

**Underinvestment and bureaucracy.** Years of fiscal caution left visible gaps: patchy mobile coverage, a slow digital state, crumbling bridges and an unreliable railway. Planning permission for infrastructure can take many years.

## Politics and power

Germany is a federal parliamentary republic. The **Chancellor** leads the government and is elected by the Bundestag, the lower house; the President is largely ceremonial. The 16 states (*Länder*) have significant powers and are represented in the Bundesrat, the upper house.

As of mid-2026, the Chancellor is **Friedrich Merz** of the centre-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), who took office in May 2025 after the February 2025 election, heading a coalition with the Bavarian CSU and the centre-left Social Democrats (SPD). The far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) came second in that election and has polled strongly since, especially in the eastern states, which complicates coalition-building and shapes the debate on immigration.

The most important economic decision of recent years came even before Merz took office. Germany's constitution contains a **debt brake** (*Schuldenbremse*), introduced in 2009, which limits the federal government's structural deficit to 0.35% of GDP. In March 2025, the outgoing Bundestag amended the constitution to exempt defence spending above 1% of GDP from that limit and to create a special fund of €500 billion for infrastructure and climate investment over twelve years. It was a striking break with decades of fiscal orthodoxy. Supporters see it as overdue investment in security and modernisation; sceptics worry about whether the money can be spent well, given planning bottlenecks, and whether it will be used to fund ordinary spending.

## The history that shaped it

Germany's economic culture is marked by two traumas. The **hyperinflation of 1923**, when prices doubled every few days and savings were wiped out, left a deep suspicion of loose money that still colours debates about the European Central Bank. The economic chaos of the early 1930s then helped bring the Nazis to power.

After the Second World War, West Germany's **Wirtschaftswunder** (economic miracle) of the 1950s was built on the currency reform of 1948, the "social market economy" championed by Ludwig Erhard, which combined free markets with a strong welfare state and worker representation, and the Marshall Plan. The Bundesbank, founded in 1957, became a byword for monetary discipline, and the Deutsche Mark one of the world's strongest currencies.

**Reunification in 1990** was a vast economic undertaking. East Germany's industry was largely uncompetitive once exposed to Western markets, and much of it closed. Transfers from West to East have amounted to well over a trillion euros. Gaps remain: wages and productivity in the eastern states are still below western levels, and many young people moved west, leaving an older population behind.

By the late 1990s, Germany was called "the sick man of Europe", with high unemployment and slow growth. The **Hartz reforms** of 2003-05 under Chancellor Gerhard Schröder loosened the labour market and tightened unemployment benefits. Combined with wage restraint and the growth of Chinese demand, they helped produce the strong 2010s. Economists still argue about how much credit the reforms deserve and how much was luck, including a euro exchange rate that was arguably weaker than a Deutsche Mark would have been.

## What to watch

- **Whether the fiscal shift works.** Can the infrastructure and defence money be spent quickly and productively enough to lift growth?
- **The car industry's transition.** Market share in electric vehicles, and the fate of suppliers, will shape industrial regions such as Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg and Lower Saxony.
- **Energy prices.** Grid expansion, renewable build-out and gas prices will determine whether energy-intensive industry stays.
- **Relations with the US and China.** Tariffs, export controls and the EU's approach to Chinese competition all matter disproportionately to Germany.
- **Politics.** The strength of the AfD and the stability of the governing coalition will influence the pace of reform.

## Key takeaways

- Germany is unusual among rich economies in how much it still manufactures, with a large surplus built on cars, machinery and chemicals sold worldwide.
- Its model relied on cheap Russian gas, a booming Chinese market and open global trade; all three have weakened since about 2022.
- The Mittelstand, vocational training and co-operative labour relations remain genuine strengths.
- The 2025 loosening of the debt brake for defence and infrastructure marks a historic change in fiscal philosophy.
- Ageing, energy costs and the electric-vehicle shift are the central long-term challenges.
