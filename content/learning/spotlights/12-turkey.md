In October 2022 Turkish consumer prices were rising at an annual rate of about 85%. Shopkeepers changed price tags weekly, families rushed to turn their wages into gold, dollars or cars, and the lira had lost most of its value against the dollar in a few years. What made the episode remarkable was that it was largely self-inflicted. While central banks around the world were raising interest rates to fight inflation, Turkey was cutting them, on the instruction of a president who argued that high interest rates cause inflation rather than cure it. Then, after the 2023 elections, came a dramatic U-turn. Turkey's story is a vivid lesson in monetary economics, but it is also the story of a large, entrepreneurial, middle-income economy that has many genuine strengths.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 86 million, with a median age in the early thirties.
- **Economy:** roughly $1.3 trillion in 2024, among the 20 largest in the world, and around $15,000 per person.
- **Biggest exports:** cars and parts, machinery, textiles and clothing, iron and steel, electrical goods, jewellery and processed food.
- **Currency:** the Turkish lira, which has lost the large majority of its value against the dollar since the early 2010s.
- **System:** an executive presidency since 2018; the country officially calls itself Türkiye.
- **Alliances:** a NATO member since 1952 and an official EU candidate since 1999, though accession talks are frozen.

## How the economy makes its money

Turkey has one of the most diversified economies among emerging markets. Services make up the largest share of output, but manufacturing is unusually important, at around a fifth of GDP. Industry is concentrated in the north-west around Istanbul, Bursa and Kocaeli, along with the Aegean around Izmir and the Anatolian "tigers", inland cities such as Gaziantep, Kayseri and Konya that grew rich on family-owned manufacturing firms from the 1980s onwards.

The car industry is the flagship. Ford, Renault, Toyota, Hyundai, Fiat (Stellantis) and others assemble vehicles in Turkey, mainly for European buyers, and a domestic electric car brand, Togg, launched in 2023. Turkey is also one of Europe's biggest producers of white goods (Arçelik, which owns the Beko brand, is a major name), a leading textile and clothing supplier to European fashion chains, and a large steelmaker using scrap metal. A fast-growing defence industry, best known for Baykar's drones, has become an export earner and a tool of foreign policy.

Construction is another big engine. Turkish contractors build airports, roads and housing at home and across the Middle East, Central Asia and Africa. At home, a huge programme of infrastructure, including a vast new Istanbul airport opened in 2018, was often financed through public-private partnerships with government revenue guarantees.

### Tourism

Tourism is a crucial earner of foreign currency. Turkey regularly ranks among the most visited countries in the world, with well over 50 million foreign visitors a year, drawn to the Mediterranean and Aegean coasts, Istanbul and Cappadocia. Tourism revenue was roughly $60 billion in 2024. Russians, Germans and Britons are the largest groups of visitors.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Turkey exported goods worth roughly $260 billion in 2024. Its biggest structural weakness is on the other side of the ledger: it imports almost all of its oil and gas, plus large quantities of intermediate goods and gold, so it tends to run a **current account deficit**, meaning it spends more abroad than it earns and must attract foreign money to fill the gap.

| Export destination | Approx. share of goods exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| European Union | ~41% |
| Germany (within EU) | ~8% |
| United Kingdom | ~6% |
| United States | ~6% |
| Iraq | ~5% |
| Russia | ~3% |

On the import side, China, Russia (mainly energy) and Germany are the largest suppliers. Turkey's trade with Russia grew strongly after 2022, as it did not join Western sanctions, and it became a large buyer of Russian oil and an important route for some goods flowing to Russia.

The key institutional link is the **customs union with the EU**, in force since 1996, which removes tariffs on industrial goods between Turkey and the EU and means Turkey adopts the EU's common external tariff. It locked Turkish factories into European supply chains. But it has flaws: it excludes agriculture and services, and Turkey must open its market to countries the EU strikes trade deals with, without automatically gaining access to theirs. Modernising it has been discussed for years but is stalled by politics.

## Strengths

- **Diversity.** A broad industrial base, big tourism sector and agriculture mean Turkey does not depend on a single commodity.
- **Location.** A bridge between Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia and Russia, and control of the Bosphorus and Dardanelles straits. As firms shorten supply chains, Turkey is a natural "nearshoring" option for Europe.
- **Entrepreneurship.** A dense network of nimble, family-owned exporters that adapt quickly to changing markets and exchange rates.
- **Demographics.** A large, relatively young workforce, though fertility has now fallen below replacement level.
- **Low public debt.** Government debt is modest, at around 25-30% of GDP in recent years, a legacy of fiscal discipline after the 2001 crisis.

## Vulnerabilities

**Inflation and credibility.** Between 2021 and 2023 the central bank cut its policy rate to 8.5% while inflation soared, and the lira collapsed. To stop people abandoning the currency, the government introduced "FX-protected deposits" in late 2021, which compensated savers from public funds if the lira fell. These were costly and have since been wound down. Inflation has fallen substantially since its peaks (about 85% in 2022 and 75% in mid-2024), but it remained well above 30% through 2025, and restoring trust in the lira takes years.

**External financing.** The current account deficit and large foreign-currency debts of Turkish companies leave the country exposed to shifts in global investor sentiment. Turks, scarred by repeated devaluations, hold much of their savings in dollars, euros and gold, and buy more whenever confidence wavers.

**Energy dependence.** Importing most of its energy means high oil and gas prices quickly widen the deficit. A Russian-built nuclear plant at Akkuyu and a Black Sea gas discovery are meant to ease this over time.

**Institutions.** Concerns about judicial independence, press freedom and the concentration of power deter some long-term foreign investors.

**Earthquakes.** On 6 February 2023, two huge earthquakes struck south-eastern Turkey, killing more than 50,000 people in the country and destroying hundreds of thousands of buildings across eleven provinces. Official estimates put the damage at more than $100 billion, around 9% of GDP. Reconstruction has added to public spending, and Istanbul faces its own long-predicted seismic risk.

## Politics and power

**Recep Tayyip Erdoğan** has dominated Turkish politics for more than two decades, first as prime minister from 2003 and then as president from 2014. A 2017 referendum created an executive presidency, in force since 2018, which abolished the post of prime minister and concentrated authority in the presidency. Erdoğan's Justice and Development Party (AKP), rooted in conservative and religious politics, governs with the nationalist MHP. He won re-election in May 2023 despite the economic turmoil.

After that victory, Erdoğan made a striking change of course. He appointed **Mehmet Şimşek**, a former Merrill Lynch economist and previously a respected finance minister, to run the economy, and new central bank leadership raised the policy rate from 8.5% to 50% by March 2024. Rates were trimmed from late 2024, sharply raised again during market turmoil in March 2025, then cut gradually. As of mid-2026, Şimşek remains finance minister and Fatih Karahan governs the central bank; the orthodox programme has held, though its durability depends on the president's continued support.

The opposition Republican People's Party (CHP) won a striking victory in the March 2024 local elections. In March 2025 Istanbul's popular CHP mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, widely seen as Erdoğan's strongest rival, was detained on corruption charges he denies, triggering mass protests and a sell-off in the lira. Critics see it as an attack on democratic competition; the government says the courts are independent. The next national elections are due by 2028. Separately, the Kurdish militant group PKK announced in 2025 that it would disarm and dissolve, a potentially historic shift after four decades of conflict.

Abroad, Turkey pursues an assertive, independent course: a NATO member that buys Russian gas, mediates between Russia and Ukraine (it brokered the 2022 Black Sea grain deal), wields influence in Syria after the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024, and has projected power from Libya to the South Caucasus.

## The history that shaped it

The Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923 by **Mustafa Kemal Atatürk** from the ruins of the Ottoman Empire. Atatürk's reforms were secular and modernising, and the economy was run on "statist" lines, with state enterprises leading industrialisation behind high tariffs.

Repeated crises followed, often involving military coups (1960, 1971, 1980). After the 1980 coup, Prime Minister Turgut Özal opened the economy to trade and foreign capital. But the 1990s were a decade of chronic high inflation, often 60-100% a year, weak coalition governments and banking fragility. In 2001 a banking and currency crisis devastated the economy. The response, led by economy minister Kemal Derviş with IMF support, created an independent central bank, cleaned up the banks and enforced fiscal discipline.

The AKP inherited those reforms when it won power in 2002 and presided over a decade of strong growth, falling inflation, rising incomes and a wave of foreign investment; in 2005 the lira dropped six zeros. From around 2013, the mood shifted: mass protests, a failed coup attempt in 2016, followed by sweeping purges, and growing pressure on the central bank. A currency crisis in 2018, sparked partly by a dispute with the United States, set the stage for the unorthodox experiment that followed.

## What to watch

- **Disinflation.** Whether inflation continues falling towards the central bank's targets.
- **Policy consistency.** Whether Erdoğan maintains support for the orthodox programme, especially as elections approach.
- **Reserves and the lira.** Rebuilding foreign-exchange reserves and persuading Turks to hold lira savings again.
- **The EU relationship.** Any move to modernise the customs union, or Turkish participation in European defence projects.
- **Politics.** The fate of İmamoğlu and the opposition, and whether the PKK peace process holds.

## Key takeaways

- Turkey is a diverse middle-income economy with strong manufacturing, tourism and construction, deeply tied to Europe through the 1996 customs union.
- Its experiment with cutting rates during high inflation drove inflation to about 85% in 2022 and crushed the lira.
- Since mid-2023 orthodox policy under Mehmet Şimşek has brought inflation down, but slowly and from a very high base.
- Energy imports, a current account deficit and fragile confidence make it vulnerable to external shocks.
- As of mid-2026 President Erdoğan dominates politics, and the treatment of the opposition is a key concern for democracy and investors alike.
