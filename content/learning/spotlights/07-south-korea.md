In the early 1960s, South Korea was poorer than Ghana. Its capital had been fought over four times during the Korean War, and its biggest exports were wigs, plywood and fish. Today its companies make the memory chips inside the world's phones and AI data centres, its shipyards build the largest gas carriers afloat, and its films, dramas and pop music are consumed from Lima to Lagos. Few countries in history have grown so fast for so long. Yet South Korea now faces a challenge that no amount of industrial prowess can easily solve: its people are having so few children that its population is set to shrink dramatically within a lifetime.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 51 million, already in gentle decline.
- **Economy:** around $1.8 trillion of output in 2024, roughly the 12th or 13th largest in the world.
- **Income:** GDP per person of around $35,000-36,000, similar to Japan and parts of southern Europe.
- **Exports:** goods and services exports worth over 40% of GDP in 2024.
- **Fertility:** about 0.75 children per woman in 2024, the lowest in the world.
- **Signature companies:** Samsung, SK Hynix, Hyundai Motor, LG, POSCO and HD Hyundai.

## How the economy makes its money

South Korea is a manufacturing and export powerhouse. Manufacturing contributes roughly a quarter of output, one of the highest shares among rich countries, and much of it is sold abroad. Services, from retail and finance to healthcare and a thriving entertainment industry, make up most of the rest, though they are generally less productive than the export champions.

The economy is dominated by the **chaebol**: sprawling, family-controlled business groups that span many industries. The largest, Samsung, makes phones, chips, ships, construction projects, insurance and much more, and its affiliates' sales are equivalent to a significant fraction of national output. SK, Hyundai and LG are the other giants. The chaebol grew up in partnership with the state, which steered cheap credit towards them in exchange for meeting export targets. They give Korea global-scale firms capable of huge, risky investments, but they also concentrate economic power, squeeze smaller suppliers and have often been entangled in political scandals.

**Semiconductors** are the crown jewel. Samsung Electronics and **SK Hynix** together produce the majority of the world's memory chips: DRAM, which computers use for working memory, and NAND flash, used for storage. The boom in artificial intelligence has made one product especially valuable: **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)**, stacked DRAM chips that sit next to AI processors such as Nvidia's. SK Hynix established an early lead as a major HBM supplier, and its profits soared. Samsung also runs a contract chipmaking (foundry) business, though it trails Taiwan's TSMC by a wide margin.

Other pillars include **cars** (Hyundai and Kia together form one of the world's largest carmakers), **shipbuilding** (Korean yards lead in high-value vessels such as LNG carriers), **batteries** for electric vehicles (LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI and SK On), **steel**, **petrochemicals**, **displays** and, increasingly, **defence equipment**, with tanks, howitzers and fighter jets sold to Poland and others. The cultural "Korean wave" (*hallyu*) of K-pop, dramas, films and beauty products has become a meaningful export in its own right and a powerful source of soft power.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

As a resource-poor country, South Korea imports almost all of its energy and raw materials, and pays for them with sophisticated manufactured goods. It usually runs a goods trade surplus, though it briefly slipped into deficit in 2022 when energy prices spiked and chip prices fell.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Semiconductors | ~21% |
| Cars | ~10% |
| Petroleum products | ~7% |
| General machinery | ~7% |
| Ships | ~4% |
| Petrochemicals | ~7% |

Imports are dominated by crude oil, natural gas and coal, along with semiconductors and components, machinery and chemicals. **China** has been Korea's largest trading partner for two decades, taking almost a fifth of exports in 2024, but the **United States** has caught up rapidly and is now about as important a market, especially for cars, batteries and machinery. **Vietnam**, where Samsung assembles many of its phones, is the third-largest market, followed by Japan, Hong Kong and Taiwan. Crude oil comes mainly from Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states, and gas and coal from Australia and Qatar.

## Strengths

- **Technological leadership** in memory chips, batteries, displays and shipbuilding, backed by some of the highest research and development spending in the world, at around 5% of GDP.
- **A highly educated workforce.** Around 70% of young adults have tertiary education, the highest share in the OECD.
- **Corporate scale.** The chaebol can fund and execute huge investments quickly.
- **Strong public finances.** Government debt is moderate, at around half of GDP, and the country holds substantial foreign exchange reserves, around $400 billion.
- **Cultural reach.** *Hallyu* supports tourism, consumer brands and Korea's global image.

## Vulnerabilities

**Demography.** South Korea's fertility rate fell to a world record low of about 0.72 in 2023 before edging up slightly in 2024 and 2025. At around a third of the replacement rate, this means each generation is far smaller than the last. Causes include punishing education costs and competition, expensive housing in Seoul, long working hours, and the burden of childcare falling on women who also want careers. Governments have spent heavily on incentives with little effect so far. Without major change, the working-age population will shrink sharply, straining pensions and growth.

**Household debt.** Korean households carry debt of roughly 90% of GDP, among the highest in the world, much of it tied to property. Many homeowners also use *jeonse*, a distinctive system in which tenants pay a large lump-sum deposit instead of monthly rent, which ties household finances closely to house prices. High debt limits spending and makes the economy sensitive to interest rates.

**Concentration.** A few firms and a single product, memory chips, account for a large share of exports and profits. The memory chip market is notoriously cyclical, with booms and busts that swing growth.

**Exposure to China and the United States.** China is both a crucial market and an increasingly fierce competitor in batteries, steel, displays, shipbuilding and even chips. The US is Korea's security guarantor and a growing market, but has used tariffs and export controls on chip technology to China, putting Korean firms in the middle. In 2025, Seoul agreed a deal with Washington that set a 15% US tariff on most Korean goods in exchange for large investment commitments in the US.

**Ageing and inequality.** Korea has one of the highest rates of old-age poverty in the OECD, a legacy of a pension system introduced late, and a sharp divide between well-paid jobs in big companies and precarious work elsewhere.

**North Korea.** The nuclear-armed North remains a constant, if usually background, security risk.

## Politics and power

South Korea is a presidential democracy. The president serves a single five-year term and holds considerable power, while the National Assembly is a single chamber elected for four years.

Korean politics has been bitterly polarised between conservatives and liberals, and the past decade has been dramatic. In December 2024, the conservative president **Yoon Suk Yeol** suddenly declared martial law, citing threats from "anti-state forces" in the opposition-led parliament. Lawmakers rushed to the National Assembly and voted to overturn the decree within hours. Yoon was impeached, removed from office by the Constitutional Court in April 2025, and subsequently put on trial.

In the snap election of June 2025, **Lee Jae-myung** of the liberal Democratic Party won the presidency. As of mid-2026, he leads a government with a majority in the National Assembly. His priorities include supporting the AI and chip industries, stimulating domestic demand, improving relations with China while maintaining the alliance with the United States, and reforming corporate governance to strengthen minority shareholders, addressing the so-called "Korea discount", the tendency for Korean shares to trade at lower valuations than peers.

## The history that shaped it

Japanese colonial rule from 1910 to 1945 was followed by division of the peninsula and the devastating **Korean War** of 1950-53. In 1961, General **Park Chung-hee** seized power and launched an export-led industrialisation drive that became known as the **"Miracle on the Han River"**, named after the river that runs through Seoul. The state directed credit to favoured firms, set export targets, and, from the 1970s, pushed into heavy and chemical industries such as steel, shipbuilding and petrochemicals. Growth averaged close to 10% a year for decades, though it came under authoritarian rule with harsh suppression of labour and dissent. Democracy arrived in 1987 after mass protests.

In 1997, the **Asian financial crisis** struck. Many chaebol had expanded recklessly on borrowed money, much of it short-term foreign debt. When foreign lenders pulled out, the won collapsed, conglomerates such as Daewoo eventually went bankrupt, and Korea needed an IMF-led rescue package of around $58 billion. Millions of citizens donated gold jewellery to help repay the debt. The IMF demanded tough reforms: bank restructuring, more open capital markets and greater corporate transparency. Unemployment soared, but recovery was swift, and the crisis reshaped Korean business, pushing it towards stronger balance sheets and global competitiveness.

The country also weathered the 2008 global financial crisis better than most. Since then it has become a technology leader, but growth has slowed to around 2% a year, typical of a mature economy, and the demographic outlook has darkened.

## What to watch

- **The chip cycle and AI demand.** How long the boom in HBM and AI-related memory lasts.
- **US trade policy and investment commitments**, and how firms balance American and Chinese markets.
- **Birth rates.** Whether the small rebound since 2024 continues.
- **Household debt and Seoul property prices**, and how the Bank of Korea balances them against growth.
- **Corporate governance reform** and whether it narrows the "Korea discount".

## Key takeaways

- South Korea's export-led industrialisation, the "Miracle on the Han River", transformed one of the world's poorest countries into a rich, high-tech economy in two generations.
- Memory chips from Samsung and SK Hynix are the heart of the economy, making it a key beneficiary of the AI boom but also vulnerable to the chip cycle.
- The 1997 crisis forced deep reforms on the chaebol and banks and still shapes economic policy.
- An ultra-low fertility rate and high household debt are the biggest long-term domestic risks.
- Caught between China and the United States, Korea must manage its two most important economic relationships at once.
