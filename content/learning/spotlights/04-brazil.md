There is an old joke, attributed to various people over the years, that "Brazil is the country of the future, and always will be." It captures a real frustration. Brazil has almost everything an economy could want: fertile land, abundant water, huge mineral and oil reserves, a population of more than 200 million and a diversified industrial base. It is the world's largest exporter of soybeans, coffee, beef, sugar and orange juice. Yet growth has repeatedly stalled, interest rates are among the highest in the world, and inequality remains stark. Understanding Brazil means understanding both its natural abundance and the long shadow of its battles with inflation.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 212 million, the largest in Latin America.
- **Economy:** about $2.2 trillion of output in 2024, the largest in Latin America and among the ten largest in the world.
- **Currency:** the real, which floats freely.
- **Interest rates:** the central bank's Selic policy rate rose to 15% in 2025, one of the highest real interest rates of any major economy.
- **Signature exports:** soybeans, crude oil, iron ore, coffee, sugar and meat.
- **Inequality:** among the most unequal large economies, with a Gini coefficient around 0.52.

## How the economy makes its money

Services account for about 70% of Brazil's output, from retail and banking to a large public sector. Industry, including mining, oil and manufacturing, contributes a little over a fifth. Agriculture directly accounts for only around 7% of GDP, but the wider **agribusiness** chain, including fertilisers, machinery, food processing, logistics and trade, is often estimated at about a quarter.

The rise of Brazilian agriculture is a remarkable story. Until the 1970s much of the vast central savannah, the **Cerrado**, was considered unsuitable for farming because of its acidic soils. Research by Embrapa, the state agricultural research agency founded in 1973, developed techniques such as liming the soil, tropical soybean varieties, and later the practice of growing two crops a year on the same land. The Cerrado became one of the world's great breadbaskets, and Brazil overtook the United States as the largest soybean producer.

Beneath the sea lies the second pillar: oil. In 2006-07, the state-controlled company **Petrobras** discovered huge reserves in the "pre-salt" layer, deep beneath the Atlantic seabed off Rio de Janeiro. Production has since grown to around 3.5 million barrels a day, making Brazil one of the world's ten largest oil producers. The third pillar is iron ore: **Vale** is one of the world's biggest miners, with vast high-grade deposits at Carajás in the Amazon state of Pará.

Brazil is not only a commodity producer. It has a substantial manufacturing sector, including cars, steel, chemicals and processed food, though much of it was built behind high tariffs and struggles to compete globally. **Embraer** is the world's third-largest maker of commercial aircraft, specialising in regional jets. Banks such as Itaú and Bradesco are highly profitable, and fintechs such as Nubank have grown rapidly, helped by **Pix**, the central bank's instant payment system, launched in 2020 and now used by most adults.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Brazil runs a solid trade surplus, driven by commodity exports, though its economy is relatively closed: trade in goods and services amounts to only around a third of GDP.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Soybeans | ~13% |
| Crude oil | ~13% |
| Iron ore | ~9% |
| Sugar | ~6% |
| Coffee | ~4% |
| Meat (beef and poultry) | ~7% |

Brazil imports fertilisers, chemicals, refined fuels, electronics, machinery and vehicle parts. **China** is by far the largest customer, buying over a quarter of Brazil's exports, especially soybeans, iron ore and oil. The **United States** takes around 12%, and is a more important buyer of manufactured goods such as aircraft and machinery. The **European Union** takes roughly 14%, and Argentina remains the most important partner within the **Mercosur** trade bloc.

Trade with the US became politically charged in 2025, when the Trump administration imposed tariffs of up to 50% on many Brazilian goods, tying them partly to the prosecution of former president Jair Bolsonaro; numerous products, including aircraft and orange juice, were exempted, and some food tariffs were later eased. Meanwhile the long-negotiated EU-Mercosur trade agreement, concluded in December 2024, has been working its way through ratification in Europe against resistance from French and other farmers.

## Strengths

- **Resource wealth.** World-class farmland, minerals and oil give Brazil export earnings that few countries can match.
- **Clean energy.** Hydropower, wind, solar and sugarcane ethanol mean that around 85-90% of Brazil's electricity comes from renewable sources.
- **A large domestic market.** Over 200 million consumers support sizeable domestic industries.
- **Strong institutions in money and payments.** An independent central bank and innovative payment infrastructure such as Pix.
- **Low foreign-currency debt.** Most public debt is in reais, and the country holds substantial foreign exchange reserves, around $350 billion, reducing the risk of a currency crisis.

## Vulnerabilities

**Commodity dependence.** Exports, the currency and government revenues all rise and fall with global prices for soy, iron ore and oil, and with demand from China.

**High interest rates and public debt.** Gross government debt is around 75-80% of GDP, high for an emerging economy. Because investors demand a premium to lend to Brazil, and because inflation has a habit of creeping up, interest rates are very high. That makes debt expensive to service and holds back private investment.

**Taxes and the "Custo Brasil".** Brazil's tax system has long been one of the most complex in the world, with overlapping federal, state and municipal taxes. A major consumption tax reform passed in 2023 will replace five taxes with a dual value added tax, phased in between 2026 and 2033. Business people also complain of poor infrastructure, red tape and labour litigation, together nicknamed the "Brazil cost".

**Low productivity growth.** Output per worker has barely grown for decades, partly due to weak education outcomes and limited competition.

**Inequality and crime.** Despite significant progress in reducing poverty, income and wealth remain very concentrated, and high levels of violent crime impose costs on businesses and households.

**The Amazon.** Deforestation threatens the rainforest's role in regulating rainfall, on which farming in the south depends, and invites trade pressure from buyers such as the EU. Deforestation fell sharply in 2023-24 after rising in the late 2010s.

## Politics and power

Brazil is a federal presidential republic. The president is both head of state and head of government, but must work with a fragmented Congress, where dozens of parties compete and a loosely organised bloc of centrist parties, known as the *Centrão*, trades support for budget resources and positions.

As of mid-2026, the president is **Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva** of the left-wing Workers' Party (PT). A former metalworker and union leader, Lula first governed from 2003 to 2010, a period of strong growth, rising minimum wages and the expansion of **Bolsa Família**, a cash transfer programme for poor families conditional on school attendance and health check-ups. He returned to office in 2023 after narrowly defeating Bolsonaro in 2022. Bolsonaro, whose supporters stormed government buildings in Brasília in January 2023, was later barred from running for office and in 2025 convicted by the Supreme Court of plotting a coup. A general election is scheduled for October 2026.

Lula's government replaced the previous spending cap with a **new fiscal framework** in 2023, which allows spending to grow faster than inflation but more slowly than revenue, alongside targets for the primary budget balance (the balance before interest payments). Markets have often doubted whether the targets will be met, and tension between the government's spending plans and the central bank's high interest rates has been a recurrent theme. The central bank, independent by law since 2021, has been led since 2025 by Gabriel Galípolo.

## The history that shaped it

Brazil's economy was built on successive commodity booms: sugar in the colonial era, gold in the eighteenth century, coffee in the nineteenth and early twentieth. It was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery, in 1888, and that legacy still shapes its inequality.

From the 1930s to the 1970s, governments pursued **import-substituting industrialisation**, building domestic industries behind tariff walls. Under the military dictatorship of 1964-85, the economy grew rapidly during the "Brazilian miracle" of the late 1960s and early 1970s, but borrowing heavily abroad to sustain growth after the oil shocks led to a debt crisis in the 1980s.

What followed was a decade and a half of chronic high inflation that peaked in the early 1990s at well over 1,000% a year, and in some years over 2,000%. Prices in supermarkets changed daily; workers rushed to spend wages before they lost value. Several plans, including price freezes and even a freeze of bank accounts in 1990, failed. The **Real Plan** of 1994, overseen by finance minister and later president Fernando Henrique Cardoso, finally succeeded. Its clever device was a temporary accounting unit, the URV, whose value was linked to the dollar, which all prices and wages were quoted in for a few months, before it was converted into a new currency, the real. This broke the habit of indexing prices to past inflation. Inflation targeting and a floating exchange rate followed in 1999.

The 2000s commodity boom, driven by China, brought rapid growth and falling poverty. Then it ended. In 2015-16 Brazil suffered one of its deepest recessions, compounded by the huge **Lava Jato** (Car Wash) corruption investigation into Petrobras and construction firms, and President Dilma Rousseff was impeached in 2016. Recovery has been modest since.

## What to watch

- **The October 2026 election** and what it means for fiscal policy, the central bank and relations with the US and China.
- **Interest rate cuts.** Whether inflation falls enough to allow the Selic to come down from its very high level.
- **Fiscal credibility.** Whether the government hits its budget targets and stabilises public debt.
- **Tax reform implementation** as the new value added tax is phased in from 2026.
- **The Amazon and climate policy**, after Brazil hosted the COP30 climate summit in Belém in November 2025.

## Key takeaways

- Brazil is an agricultural and mineral superpower, exporting soybeans, oil and iron ore, with China as its largest customer.
- The memory of hyperinflation, defeated by the 1994 Real Plan, still shapes its very high interest rates and cautious central bank.
- Public debt is high for an emerging economy, and fiscal credibility is a constant source of market anxiety.
- Deep inequality has been reduced by programmes such as Bolsa Família but remains a defining feature of the country.
- The Amazon matters both for the global climate and for Brazil's own farming and trade relationships.
