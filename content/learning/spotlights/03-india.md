On a street corner in almost any Indian town, a vegetable seller with a cart and no shop will hold up a laminated QR code. Customers pay a few rupees by scanning it on their phones, and the money arrives instantly. India now processes more real-time digital payments than any other country, many of them for tiny sums. The scene captures the country's economy in miniature: cutting-edge technology layered onto a vast informal economy of small traders, farmers and casual workers. India has overtaken China as the world's most populous nation and is now one of its five largest economies. But it is still a lower-middle-income country, and its central challenge is turning headline growth into decent jobs for everyone.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 1.45 billion, the largest in the world since 2023, with a median age under 30.
- **Economy:** around $4 trillion of output in 2025, roughly fifth in the world in dollar terms and third when adjusted for local prices.
- **Income:** GDP per person of around $2,700 in 2024, about a fifth of China's.
- **Growth:** typically 6-8% a year since the pandemic, the fastest among large economies.
- **Currency:** the Indian rupee, managed by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).
- **Signature industries:** IT and business services, pharmaceuticals, refined petroleum, chemicals, and a fast-growing electronics assembly sector.

## How the economy makes its money

Most countries that grew rich did so by moving people from farms into factories, then into services. India took an unusual route. **Services** now generate more than half of gross value added, while **manufacturing** has stubbornly hovered around 15-17% of output for decades, and **agriculture** contributes roughly 16-18%.

The star of the services story is information technology. Firms such as Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech grew from the 1990s by writing code and running back-office operations for Western companies, exploiting a pool of English-speaking engineers and the time-zone difference that allowed work to continue overnight. Today India exports well over $350 billion of services a year, and is home to more than 1,500 **global capability centres**, in-house hubs where multinationals such as banks, retailers and chipmakers run engineering, analytics and research. Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune are their heartlands.

Yet the IT sector employs only a few million people directly, in a labour force of more than 500 million. The picture of employment looks very different from the picture of output. Agriculture still employs around 45% of workers, many on tiny plots that produce low incomes. Construction absorbs millions more. And a very large share of workers, commonly estimated at over 80%, are **informal**: they have no written contract, no social security and no paid leave, whether as street vendors, domestic helpers, rickshaw drivers or workers in small workshops.

The government has tried to boost manufacturing through the **Production Linked Incentive (PLI)** schemes launched in 2020, which pay firms a percentage of their extra sales in sectors such as mobile phones, pharmaceuticals, solar modules and automotive components. The most visible success is smartphones: Apple's suppliers, including Foxconn and Tata Electronics, have rapidly expanded iPhone assembly in India, and phones have become one of the country's largest exports. Critics point out that much of the value in these devices is still imported, and that assembly creates fewer jobs than older labour-intensive industries such as textiles and footwear, where Bangladesh and Vietnam have often outcompeted India.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

India runs a persistent deficit in goods, importing considerably more than it exports, and a large surplus in services and in remittances, the money sent home by Indians working abroad, which at well over $100 billion a year are the largest of any country.

| Main goods exports (FY2024-25, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Engineering goods (machinery, vehicles, metals) | ~25% |
| Refined petroleum products | ~15% |
| Electronics (mainly smartphones) | ~9% |
| Gems and jewellery | ~7% |
| Pharmaceuticals | ~7% |
| Textiles and garments | ~8% |

The biggest imports are crude oil, which accounts for roughly a quarter of the import bill, along with gold, electronics, coal and machinery. The **United States** is the largest export market, taking almost a fifth of goods exports. **China** is the largest source of imports, supplying electronics, chemicals, solar components and machinery, leaving India with a large bilateral deficit and a wary relationship. The United Arab Emirates, the Netherlands, Saudi Arabia and Russia are also major partners; Russia became a leading supplier of discounted crude after 2022.

That Russian oil became a flashpoint in 2025, when the US imposed tariffs totalling 50% on many Indian goods, half of it explicitly as a penalty for those purchases. The episode strained relations and gave fresh urgency to India's trade diplomacy, including a free trade agreement with the UK signed in July 2025 and long-running negotiations with the EU.

## Strengths

- **Demographics.** India has the world's largest working-age population, and it will keep growing for another two decades.
- **Digital public infrastructure.** The state-built "India Stack" combines **Aadhaar**, a biometric identity system covering over a billion people, the **UPI** instant payments network, and tools for sharing documents and data. It has slashed the cost of opening bank accounts, paying welfare benefits and making payments.
- **Services excellence.** A deep pool of engineers and managers makes India a global back office and increasingly a research hub.
- **Infrastructure push.** Public investment has transformed highways, airports, ports and rail freight corridors over the past decade.
- **Macroeconomic stability.** Inflation targeting since 2016, large foreign exchange reserves and a cleaned-up banking system leave India less fragile than in past decades.

## Vulnerabilities

**Jobs.** Each year millions of young people enter the labour market, many with degrees, and too few find formal, well-paid work. Youth unemployment among graduates is high, and competition for government jobs is ferocious. Women's participation in paid work has risen in recent surveys but remains low by international standards.

**Agriculture.** Farm incomes are low and vulnerable to the monsoon and to rising temperatures. Attempts at reform are politically explosive: three laws to liberalise agricultural markets were repealed in 2021 after a year of farmer protests.

**Human capital.** Despite progress, many children leave school without basic literacy and numeracy, and malnutrition remains widespread.

**Energy and climate.** India depends on imported oil and still generates most of its electricity from coal, though solar capacity is expanding rapidly. Extreme heat, floods and air pollution impose large economic costs.

**State capacity and regulation.** Contract enforcement is slow, land acquisition is difficult, and frequent changes in rules, including tariffs, can deter investors.

## Politics and power

India is a federal parliamentary democracy, with power shared between the central government in New Delhi and 28 states, which control key areas such as land, agriculture, policing and much of health and education. Elections, conducted by an independent Election Commission, involve almost a billion registered voters.

As of mid-2026, the prime minister is **Narendra Modi** of the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), in office since 2014. In the 2024 general election the BJP lost its outright majority in the lower house, and Modi now governs through the National Democratic Alliance, relying on regional partners including the Telugu Desam Party and Janata Dal (United). The main opposition force is the Indian National Congress, led in parliament by Rahul Gandhi.

Modi's economic record includes the **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** of 2017, which replaced a patchwork of state and central taxes with a single national system (simplified further in 2025), a new bankruptcy code, the big infrastructure drive and the expansion of digital welfare payments. It also includes the 2016 **demonetisation**, which abruptly withdrew most banknotes in circulation and caused significant disruption to the cash-based informal economy. Supporters credit him with decisive leadership and stronger state delivery; critics point to centralised power, pressure on the press and civil society, and religious polarisation.

## The history that shaped it

At independence in 1947, India was poor and mostly agrarian after two centuries of colonial rule. Its first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, pursued a mixed economy with heavy industry in state hands, five-year plans and protection from imports. The system that grew from this, nicknamed the **Licence Raj**, required permits for almost any business decision. Growth averaged around 3.5% a year, the so-called "Hindu rate of growth", a phrase coined by the economist Raj Krishna.

The 1960s **Green Revolution**, built on high-yielding seeds, irrigation and fertiliser, turned India from a country dependent on food aid into one that could feed itself.

In 1991, a balance of payments crisis left India with foreign exchange reserves sufficient for only a few weeks of imports, and it had to pledge gold to raise emergency loans. The government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as finance minister, responded with sweeping **liberalisation**: dismantling industrial licensing, cutting tariffs, devaluing the rupee and opening up to foreign investment. Growth accelerated, and the IT boom took off soon afterwards.

The 2000s brought rapid growth, including several years near 9%, followed by a hangover of bad loans in state-owned banks after an infrastructure lending boom. Cleaning up those balance sheets took most of the 2010s. The pandemic hit India hard in 2020-21, with a sharp contraction followed by a strong rebound.

## What to watch

- **Manufacturing jobs.** Whether electronics, semiconductors and labour-intensive industries can absorb young workers at scale.
- **Trade relations with the US.** The outcome of tariff negotiations matters for exporters of textiles, jewellery, seafood and machinery.
- **Private investment.** Public spending has driven investment; the hope is for companies to take the baton.
- **Monsoon and food inflation.** Weather still swings inflation and rural spending.
- **State elections.** Results in large states shape the central government's room to manoeuvre.

## Key takeaways

- India grew rich in services first, especially IT and business outsourcing, rather than following the classic factory-led path of East Asia.
- Most workers remain in agriculture or informal jobs, so headline growth has not translated evenly into good employment.
- The 1991 liberalisation was the turning point that ended the Licence Raj and opened the economy.
- Digital public infrastructure such as Aadhaar and UPI is a genuine global innovation.
- The demographic window is large but will not stay open forever; creating jobs is the decisive test.
