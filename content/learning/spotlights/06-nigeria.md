In the space of a few weeks in mid-2023, two decisions transformed daily life for more than 200 million Nigerians. The newly inaugurated president announced that "fuel subsidy is gone", and petrol prices at the pump roughly tripled almost overnight. Days later, the central bank began dismantling the system that had held the naira at an artificially strong official rate, and the currency began a fall that would see it lose around two-thirds of its value against the dollar. Economists had urged both reforms for years. For ordinary households, they brought the worst cost-of-living crisis in a generation. Nigeria's story today is about whether this hard medicine can finally cure an economy that has long underperformed its enormous potential.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 230 million, the largest in Africa and the sixth largest in the world, with a median age of around 18.
- **Economy:** after a statistical rebasing in 2025, output was put at about ₦370 trillion for 2024, roughly $240-250 billion at market exchange rates, among the four largest economies in Africa.
- **Income:** GDP per person of roughly $1,000 at market exchange rates in 2024, much lower in dollar terms than a decade earlier.
- **Oil:** Africa's largest or second-largest crude producer, typically pumping around 1.5-1.7 million barrels a day.
- **Currency:** the naira, now trading much more freely after the 2023 reforms.
- **Signature sectors:** oil and gas, agriculture, trade, telecoms, fintech and film.

## How the economy makes its money

Nigeria's economy is often described as an oil economy, but that is only half true. Oil and gas typically account for only around 5-10% of GDP. The bulk of output comes from **agriculture**, which contributes roughly a fifth to a quarter and employs about a third of workers, from smallholders growing cassava, yams, maize and rice to cocoa and sesame producers; **trade**, the vast world of markets, shops and traders; and a growing services sector including telecoms, finance, real estate and entertainment.

Yet oil matters far more than its share of GDP suggests. It typically provides around 85-90% of export earnings and, historically, around half of government revenue. When oil prices or output fall, Nigeria runs short of dollars, the currency comes under pressure, and the government struggles to pay its bills. Nigeria's tax take is among the lowest in the world, at roughly 10% of GDP, which leaves the state chronically underfunded.

The informal economy is enormous. Most workers are self-employed or work in small family businesses without formal contracts or tax registration.

Then there is the energy and creativity of **Lagos**, a sprawling megacity of perhaps 15-20 million people. It is home to Africa's most vibrant start-up scene, particularly in **fintech**: firms such as Flutterwave, Moniepoint and OPay process vast volumes of payments, and Paystack was bought by the US payments company Stripe in 2020. **Nollywood**, Nigeria's film industry, is one of the most prolific in the world by number of titles, and Afrobeats artists such as Burna Boy, Wizkid and Tems have found global audiences. These industries are economically modest but culturally powerful, and they hint at what a young, connected population can do.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

For decades, Nigeria exported crude oil and imported refined fuels, because its own state-owned refineries barely worked. This absurdity, a major oil producer importing petrol, was one of the starkest symbols of the economy's problems.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Crude oil | ~75-80% |
| Liquefied and other natural gas | ~10% |
| Cocoa and products | ~4% |
| Fertilisers (urea) | ~2% |
| Other (sesame, cashews, metals and more) | ~5-8% |

Crude oil is sold widely, with **India**, **Spain**, the **Netherlands**, **France** and the **United States** among the main buyers. Imports, including machinery, vehicles, food such as wheat, refined petroleum and consumer goods, come especially from **China**, which supplies roughly a quarter, as well as Belgium and the Netherlands (often as transit hubs for European goods), India and the US.

That picture is changing. The **Dangote refinery** near Lagos, built by Aliko Dangote, Africa's richest man, began operating in 2024 and has a design capacity of about 650,000 barrels a day, making it one of the largest single-site refineries in the world. Once running at full capacity, it could cover Nigeria's petrol needs and export to the rest of West Africa, saving billions of dollars of imports a year. Its ramp-up has been accompanied by disputes over crude supply, pricing and import licences, a reminder of how politically charged fuel remains.

## Strengths

- **Demography and market size.** A huge and young population means a growing labour force and consumer market; Nigeria's population could exceed 350 million by 2050 on UN projections.
- **Entrepreneurship.** A dynamic culture of trading and business-building, visible from Lagos markets to tech hubs.
- **Natural resources.** Large reserves of oil and especially gas, which could supply domestic power and industry, along with fertile land and solid minerals.
- **The diaspora.** Nigerians abroad send home around $20 billion a year in remittances, and bring skills, investment and networks.
- **Regional weight.** Nigeria is the anchor of the Economic Community of West African States and the African Continental Free Trade Area.

## Vulnerabilities

**Inflation and poverty.** The reforms of 2023 drove inflation above 30% by 2024, eroding living standards. Food prices were particularly painful. The World Bank estimates that a large share of Nigerians live below the national poverty line. A rebased consumer price index in 2025 and a more stable naira brought measured inflation down, but prices remain high relative to incomes.

**Security.** Nigeria faces several overlapping challenges: the jihadist insurgencies of Boko Haram and the Islamic State West Africa Province in the north-east; criminal "bandit" gangs carrying out kidnappings for ransom in the north-west; violent clashes between farmers and herders in the central belt; separatist tensions in the south-east; and oil theft and pipeline vandalism in the Niger Delta. Insecurity disrupts farming, raises food prices, deters investment and has reduced oil output.

**Power shortages.** Electricity supply is notoriously unreliable. Businesses and households rely on costly diesel and petrol generators, making Nigerian production expensive.

**Oil sector underperformance.** Output has repeatedly fallen short of Nigeria's capacity and its OPEC quota because of theft, underinvestment and the exit of international oil majors from onshore fields.

**Debt service.** Public debt is moderate as a share of GDP, but because revenues are so low, interest payments absorb a very large share of government income.

**Jobs.** Unemployment and underemployment among young people are very high, driving emigration among skilled professionals, a trend Nigerians call *japa*.

## Politics and power

Nigeria is a federal republic with a presidential system modelled loosely on that of the United States, with 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory of Abuja. Its politics are shaped by the balance between the mainly Muslim north and the mainly Christian south and by a large number of ethnic groups, the biggest being the Hausa-Fulani, Yoruba and Igbo. An informal convention rotates power between north and south.

As of mid-2026, the president is **Bola Ahmed Tinubu** of the All Progressives Congress (APC), a former governor of Lagos State who won the 2023 election. His administration launched the fuel subsidy removal and currency reforms, and followed with a broad overhaul of the tax system signed into law in 2025 and largely effective from 2026. The central bank, under governor Olayemi Cardoso since 2023, raised interest rates sharply to tame inflation and cleared a backlog of foreign currency obligations, restoring a degree of investor confidence. The next general election is due in early 2027, and the political calendar increasingly colours debates about reform and its costs.

## The history that shaped it

Nigeria was created as a single colony by the British in 1914, joining together very different northern and southern territories. It gained independence in 1960. Oil was first found in commercial quantities in 1956 in the Niger Delta, and by the 1970s it had become the dominant export.

The years after independence were turbulent: coups in 1966 and the **Biafran war** of 1967-70, when the south-east tried to secede, which killed perhaps a million people or more, many through famine. The 1970s oil boom brought huge revenues and ambitious spending, but also neglect of agriculture and manufacturing, a classic case of what economists call **"Dutch disease"**, where a resource boom strengthens the currency and crowds out other exports.

Military rule dominated until 1999, with widespread corruption and economic mismanagement. Since the return to civilian rule, Nigeria has held regular elections and seen several peaceful transfers of power, including in 2015 when an incumbent president lost and conceded for the first time. In 2005-06 Nigeria secured large debt relief from the Paris Club of creditors. A statistical rebasing in 2014 nearly doubled measured GDP and briefly made Nigeria Africa's largest economy.

But the oil price crash of 2014-16 and the pandemic exposed the old model's flaws. Nigeria fell into recession in 2016 and again in 2020. Successive governments defended the naira with multiple exchange rates and used scarce dollars to subsidise petrol, which by 2022 was consuming a very large share of federal revenue and mostly benefited better-off households and smugglers who sold cheap fuel across the border. These were the distortions that the 2023 reforms set out to end.

## What to watch

- **Inflation and the naira.** Whether the currency stays stable and inflation falls enough to restore real incomes.
- **Oil production** and whether security improvements and new investment raise output towards capacity.
- **Dangote and refining.** Whether the refinery reaches full capacity and ends the dependence on imported petrol.
- **Tax reform** and whether it raises revenue without hurting small businesses.
- **The 2027 elections** and whether reform momentum survives the campaign.

## Key takeaways

- Oil is only a modest share of Nigeria's GDP but dominates exports and government revenue, making the economy highly exposed to oil prices.
- The 2023 removal of the fuel subsidy and liberalisation of the naira were long overdue but caused a severe cost-of-living crisis.
- A young and fast-growing population is both Nigeria's greatest asset and its biggest challenge, given the shortage of good jobs.
- Insecurity, unreliable power and very low tax revenues are deep structural constraints.
- Lagos's tech, music and film industries show the creative and entrepreneurial potential that reforms aim to unlock.
