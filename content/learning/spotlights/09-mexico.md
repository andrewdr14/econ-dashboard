Stand on the border crossing at Nuevo Laredo on a weekday morning and you will see one of the busiest trade arteries on earth: a queue of lorries carrying car parts, televisions, fridges, avocados and beer into Texas. In 2023 Mexico overtook China to become the largest supplier of goods to the United States, and in a typical year more than four-fifths of everything Mexico exports heads north. This is the country's great economic bet: that being next door to the world's richest consumer market is the best asset a developing economy can have. It has paid off handsomely, but it also means that when Washington changes its mind about trade, Mexico feels it first.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 130 million, the world's largest Spanish-speaking country.
- **Economy:** roughly $1.8-1.9 trillion in 2024, among the 15 largest in the world.
- **Income:** about $14,000 per person in 2024, upper-middle income, with deep regional inequality between the industrial north and the poorer south.
- **Biggest exports:** cars and car parts, computers and electronics, electrical machinery, oil, fresh produce.
- **Currency:** the Mexican peso, freely floating and one of the most traded emerging-market currencies.
- **System:** a federal presidential republic of 32 states; presidents serve one six-year term (the *sexenio*) with no re-election.

## How the economy makes its money

Mexico has three economies living side by side. The first is the **export manufacturing belt** in the north and the central Bajío region, around cities such as Monterrey, Saltillo, Querétaro and Guanajuato. Here, factories (many of them *maquiladoras*, plants that import components duty-free, assemble them and re-export) are woven into North American supply chains. A pickup truck may cross the border several times as parts are machined, assembled and fitted. Mexico is one of the world's largest vehicle producers and the largest exporter of flat-screen televisions to the US.

The second is a large **domestic services economy**: retail, tourism (Cancún, Mexico City, the Pacific coast), finance, telecoms and transport. Tourism alone brings in tens of millions of international visitors a year.

The third is the **informal economy**: street vendors, small family businesses and unregistered workers. Over half of Mexican workers are informal, without formal contracts or social security. Informality helps explain why productivity has grown so slowly overall: the modern export sector is highly productive, but most people do not work in it.

Two more sources of money are worth singling out. **Remittances**, money sent home by Mexicans working abroad, almost all in the US, reached a record of roughly $65 billion in 2024, more than 3% of GDP and a lifeline for poorer states such as Michoacán, Guanajuato and Chiapas. Flows softened in 2025 as US immigration enforcement tightened. And **oil**, once the backbone of public finances, still matters, though far less than it did.

### Pemex: the national champion in trouble

Petróleos Mexicanos (Pemex) is a symbol of national sovereignty: President Lázaro Cárdenas expropriated foreign oil companies in 1938, and the anniversary is still celebrated. But the company is in poor shape. Crude output has fallen by roughly half from its 2004 peak of about 3.4 million barrels a day as the giant Cantarell field declined. Pemex carries financial debts of roughly $100 billion, the largest of any oil company in the world, plus large unpaid bills to suppliers. The previous government poured money into it, including a new refinery at Dos Bocas in Tabasco, and the current one has continued to support it with capital injections and debt operations. Pemex is effectively a contingent liability for the Mexican state: if it cannot pay, the Treasury will.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Mexico is the most trade-dependent large economy in the Americas, with exports and imports together worth around 70-80% of GDP. Total goods exports were roughly $600 billion in 2024. The pattern is strikingly lopsided: it sells to the United States and buys components from both the US and Asia.

| Partner | Share of exports (2024, approx.) | Share of imports (2024, approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| United States | ~83% | ~40% |
| Canada | ~3% | ~2% |
| China | ~1-2% | ~20% |
| European Union | ~4% | ~10% |

That gap with China is politically sensitive. Mexico runs a large trade deficit with China, importing parts and materials that end up inside goods exported to the US. Washington has grown suspicious that Mexico is a "back door" for Chinese goods, and Mexico has responded by moving to raise tariffs on imports from countries with which it has no trade agreement, China foremost among them.

Manufactured goods make up close to 90% of exports; vehicles and parts alone are roughly a quarter. Agriculture is small in GDP terms but significant in trade: Mexico is the world's leading exporter of avocados, a major supplier of tomatoes and berries, and the US's largest source of imported beer.

## Strengths

- **Location.** Sharing a 3,000-kilometre border with the US is an enormous advantage, and it underpins the **nearshoring** story: firms trying to reduce their reliance on China have expanded factories in Mexico, particularly in the north.
- **Trade agreements.** Beyond the USMCA, Mexico has more free-trade deals than almost any country, including with the EU and Japan and through the CPTPP Pacific pact.
- **Macroeconomic credibility.** The Bank of Mexico (Banxico) is independent and well respected, the peso floats freely, and public debt, at around half of GDP, is moderate by international standards. Mexico has kept investment-grade credit ratings.
- **Young workforce.** A median age around 30 and a large pool of engineering graduates support manufacturing.
- **Manufacturing depth.** Decades of integration have produced skilled suppliers, especially in cars and aerospace.

## Vulnerabilities

**Dependence on one customer.** With over four-fifths of exports going to the US, Mexico's growth tracks the American business cycle and American politics. In 2025 the Trump administration imposed tariffs on Mexico citing fentanyl and migration, while exempting goods that comply with USMCA rules; sector tariffs on steel, aluminium and cars hit harder. The exemptions softened the blow, but the uncertainty chilled investment just as nearshoring was meant to take off.

**Slow growth.** Despite its advantages, Mexico has averaged only around 2% annual growth for three decades, and the economy came close to stalling in 2025. Low investment, informality, weak infrastructure and patchy rule of law hold it back.

**Narrow tax base.** Mexico collects only around 17% of GDP in taxes, among the lowest in the OECD. That leaves little room to spend on education, health and security while also supporting Pemex and generous social programmes.

**Energy bottlenecks.** Recent governments favoured the state power company (CFE) and Pemex over private investors. Critics argue this has slowed investment in electricity grids and renewables just as new factories need reliable power.

**Security.** Organised crime is pervasive. Homicides have run at roughly 30,000 a year, cartels extort businesses and control territory in parts of the country, and in 2025 the US designated several cartels as terrorist organisations. Insecurity raises business costs and deters investment, especially outside the industrial heartlands.

## Politics and power

Mexican politics has been transformed by **Morena**, the party founded by Andrés Manuel López Obrador ("AMLO"), president from 2018 to 2024. AMLO combined fiscal caution with big cash transfers to pensioners, students and farmers, raised the minimum wage sharply and attacked independent regulators. He remained hugely popular.

His successor, **Claudia Sheinbaum**, a climate scientist and former mayor of Mexico City, won the June 2024 election by a landslide, with about 60% of the vote, becoming Mexico's first woman president. As of mid-2026, Morena and its allies hold large majorities in both houses of Congress, enough to amend the constitution. Sheinbaum has a more technocratic style than her mentor and has generally been praised for managing relations with the Trump administration calmly, cooperating on migration and security while avoiding a tariff war.

The most contested change has been the **judicial reform**, passed in September 2024, which replaced appointed judges, including the Supreme Court, with judges elected by popular vote. The first judicial elections took place in June 2025 with very low turnout. Supporters say it democratises a corrupt, elitist judiciary. Critics, including business groups and many legal scholars, argue it politicises the courts, weakens checks on a dominant ruling party and raises the risks for investors who may need to enforce contracts against the state. Alongside the abolition of several autonomous regulators, it amounts to a significant concentration of power.

The opposition, led by the once-dominant PRI and the centre-right PAN, is weak and divided. Mid-term elections are due in 2027.

## The history that shaped it

The Mexican Revolution (1910-1920) produced a political settlement that lasted most of the 20th century: rule by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI), which governed without interruption for 71 years. In the post-war decades Mexico followed **import substitution**, protecting domestic industry behind tariffs, and grew strongly in what was called the "Mexican miracle".

The oil boom of the 1970s ended in disaster. When oil prices fell and US interest rates soared, Mexico announced in 1982 that it could not service its foreign debt, triggering the Latin American debt crisis and a "lost decade". The response was a sharp turn to open markets: privatisation, joining the GATT in 1986 and, in 1994, the **North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)** with the US and Canada.

NAFTA's first year coincided with the "tequila crisis", a peso collapse at the end of 1994 that required a US-led rescue. But the agreement transformed northern Mexico into an export powerhouse. Then came a second shock: China's entry into the WTO in 2001, which took market share in labour-intensive goods. Politically, the PRI lost the presidency in 2000 to Vicente Fox of the PAN, a peaceful transfer that marked Mexico's arrival as a multiparty democracy. The drug war launched in 2006 under Felipe Calderón ushered in a long period of high violence.

During his first term, Donald Trump forced a renegotiation of NAFTA, which became the **United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA)** in 2020, with tougher rules for cars (more North American content, minimum wage requirements for some production) and a clause requiring a joint review after six years.

## What to watch

- **The USMCA review.** The formal joint review falls due in 2026. If all three countries agree, the pact is extended; if not, it moves to annual reviews and could eventually lapse. As of mid-2026 the process is under way and its outcome is the single biggest economic question for Mexico.
- **Tariffs and China.** Whether Mexico's tougher stance on Chinese imports satisfies Washington.
- **Nearshoring investment.** Whether factory announcements turn into actual construction once trade rules are clearer.
- **Pemex.** Whether the company's finances stabilise or it needs ever more support from the Treasury.
- **The courts.** How the newly elected judiciary rules in cases involving the government and investors.
- **The peso.** It strengthened strongly in 2023-24 (the "super peso") and has been volatile since; interest rate cuts by Banxico narrow its appeal to carry traders.

## Key takeaways

- Mexico's economy is built on integration with the US: over 80% of exports go north, and the USMCA, under review in 2026, is its foundation.
- Remittances of roughly $65 billion a year and a sizeable manufacturing base, especially cars and electronics, are major strengths.
- Pemex is a heavily indebted national champion that has become a burden on public finances.
- Slow growth, informality, insecurity and a narrow tax base hold the economy back from its potential.
- As of mid-2026 President Sheinbaum enjoys strong majorities, but the judicial reform has raised questions about checks and balances.
