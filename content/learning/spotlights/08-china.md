In 1978 China's economy was roughly the size of the Netherlands', and most of its people worked the land for little more than subsistence. Today it makes close to a third of the world's manufactured goods and runs a trade surplus approaching a trillion dollars a year. No large economy in history has grown so fast for so long. Yet the model that delivered this miracle, pouring savings into factories, flats and infrastructure, is showing its age. Understanding China today means understanding both the machine it built and the problems that machine now creates.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 1.41 billion, shrinking since 2022 and ageing quickly.
- **Economy:** the world's second largest at market exchange rates (roughly $18-19 trillion in 2024) and the largest measured by purchasing power.
- **Income:** around $13,000 per person in 2024, placing it in the World Bank's upper-middle-income group.
- **Biggest exports:** electronics, machinery, electrical equipment, vehicles and batteries, steel, textiles.
- **Currency:** the renminbi ("people's currency"), whose unit is the yuan; tightly managed and not freely convertible.
- **System:** a one-party state led by the Chinese Communist Party, with a mixed economy in which the state retains the commanding heights.

## How the economy makes its money

The simplest way to describe China's growth model is that it saves a great deal and invests most of what it saves. For much of the past two decades, investment (spending on factories, machinery, housing, roads, railways and power grids) has been around 40% of GDP, extraordinarily high by international standards; in most rich countries the figure is closer to 20%. Household consumption, by contrast, has hovered at roughly 40% of GDP, far below the 55-70% typical elsewhere.

That investment went into three big buckets. The first was **manufacturing**. China combines vast supply chains, dense industrial clusters (the Pearl River Delta around Shenzhen and Guangzhou, the Yangtze River Delta around Shanghai), excellent logistics and a deep pool of engineers. The result is a manufacturing sector that produces more than the next several countries combined.

The second was **infrastructure**. China has built the world's largest high-speed rail network, tens of thousands of kilometres of motorways, new airports and ports, much of it financed by local governments.

The third, and the most troublesome, was **property**. From the late 1990s, when urban housing was privatised, flats became the main store of wealth for Chinese families. At its peak around 2020, property and the industries that feed it (steel, cement, glass, furniture, appliances) accounted, on some economists' estimates, for something like a quarter of all economic activity. Local governments, which rely heavily on selling land-use rights to developers, became addicted to the land boom.

Services now make up a little over half of GDP, with giant platforms in e-commerce and payments. But the economy's centre of gravity remains industrial, and Beijing likes it that way: officials talk of "new quality productive forces", meaning advanced manufacturing and technology, rather than a shift towards consumption-led growth.

### The "new three"

As property faded, the government steered credit towards what it calls the "new three" industries: electric vehicles, lithium-ion batteries and solar cells. China now dominates all three. Its carmakers, led by BYD, have turned the country into the world's largest car exporter within a few years. Chinese firms produce the large majority of the world's solar modules and battery cells and refine most of the world's lithium, cobalt and rare earths. It is a remarkable achievement, but the sheer volume has driven prices down worldwide, prompting complaints of "overcapacity" abroad and price wars at home that Beijing calls "involution" and has tried to curb.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

China is the world's largest exporter of goods, selling roughly $3.5 trillion in 2024, and its goods trade surplus reached close to $1 trillion that year. It sells finished and intermediate manufactures and buys raw materials, energy, food and some high-end components, especially semiconductors, which are its single largest import category.

The United States remains the largest single-country buyer, but its share has fallen steadily since the trade war began in 2018, while South-East Asia, the Middle East, Latin America and Africa have taken more.

| Export destination | Approx. share of China's exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| ASEAN (South-East Asia) | ~16% |
| European Union | ~15% |
| United States | ~15% |
| Hong Kong | ~8% |
| Japan and South Korea combined | ~9% |

Some exports to South-East Asia and Mexico are components assembled there and shipped on to America, so China depends on US demand more than the table suggests. On the import side, China is the dominant buyer of iron ore (much of it from Australia and Brazil), the largest importer of crude oil, and a major buyer of soybeans from Brazil and the US.

## Strengths

- **Scale and supply chains.** No other country can match China's breadth of suppliers, which lets firms move from prototype to mass production faster and more cheaply than anywhere else.
- **Engineering talent.** China graduates millions of science and engineering students each year and now leads in many areas of applied research, from batteries to telecoms.
- **Policy firepower.** The state controls the big banks and can direct credit towards priorities at speed. The national government's own debt is moderate, and the country is a net creditor to the rest of the world.
- **Energy transition position.** China is both the largest emitter of greenhouse gases and the biggest builder of renewable power; it installs more solar and wind capacity each year than the rest of the world combined.
- **Domestic savings.** High saving means China funds itself rather than relying on foreign borrowing.

## Vulnerabilities

**The property bust.** In 2020 regulators introduced the "three red lines", limits on developers' borrowing. That pricked the bubble. Evergrande, once the world's most indebted developer, defaulted in 2021 and was ordered to be liquidated by a Hong Kong court in 2024; Country Garden and many others followed it into distress. New home sales and construction starts have fallen sharply from their peaks, prices have slid for several years, and millions of pre-sold flats were left unfinished. With so much household wealth in property, falling prices sap confidence.

**Local government debt.** Local governments borrowed heavily through "local government financing vehicles" (LGFVs), companies that sit off the official balance sheet. Estimates of this hidden debt vary widely but run to tens of trillions of yuan. In late 2024 Beijing announced a multi-year programme worth around 10 trillion yuan to swap hidden debt into cheaper official bonds. That eases the pressure but does not fix the underlying issue: local governments have big spending responsibilities and limited tax revenues, and land sales have dried up.

**Deflationary pressure.** Weak demand at home and abundant supply have kept consumer price inflation close to zero and factory-gate prices falling for an extended period since 2022. Falling prices make debts harder to service and encourage firms to export their way out, which deepens trade tensions.

**Demographics.** The legacy of the one-child policy (1980-2015) is a population that peaked in 2021 and is now falling, with births well under half their level of the mid-2010s. The working-age population is shrinking and the retirement age, among the lowest in the world, began a gradual rise in 2025. China risks, as the saying goes, growing old before it grows rich.

**Trade friction.** In 2025 the United States raised tariffs on Chinese goods to extraordinary levels before a series of truces brought them down; China responded with its own tariffs and, pointedly, with controls on exports of rare earths and related magnets. The European Union has imposed duties on Chinese electric cars, and other countries from Mexico to Turkey have raised barriers. An export-led model needs willing buyers.

**Technology controls.** US restrictions on advanced chips and chipmaking equipment have pushed China towards self-reliance, with real progress, but the most advanced chips remain a weak point.

## Politics and power

China is governed by the Chinese Communist Party, whose ultimate authority sits with the seven-man Politburo Standing Committee. As of mid-2026, **Xi Jinping** holds the three top posts: General Secretary of the party, chairman of the Central Military Commission and state president. In 2018 the constitutional two-term limit on the presidency was removed, and in 2022 he began a third term as party leader, breaking the pattern of orderly succession that had held since the 1990s. **Li Qiang**, a long-time Xi ally who previously ran Shanghai, has been premier since 2023 and oversees day-to-day economic management.

There are no competitive national elections. The National People's Congress, which meets each March, approves laws, budgets and five-year plans. The 15th Five-Year Plan, covering 2026-2030, emphasises technological self-reliance, advanced manufacturing and, more cautiously than many economists would like, raising household consumption.

The tone of policy has changed under Xi. The state sector has been strengthened; the private sector, which produces most of China's output, jobs and innovation, went through a bruising period in 2020-2022, when regulators cracked down on internet platforms, private tutoring and fintech (Ant Group's stock market listing was halted in 2020). Since 2023 the government has tried to reassure private entrepreneurs, passing a law to promote the private economy in 2025, but confidence has been slow to return. National security now features prominently in economic decision-making.

### The renminbi

The renminbi is not freely floating. Each morning the central bank, the People's Bank of China, sets a reference rate, and the currency may move only within a band around it. Capital controls limit how much money households and firms can move abroad. Beijing has promoted the renminbi for trade invoicing and built its own cross-border payment system, and its use has grown, but it still accounts for only a small share of global payments and central bank reserves compared with the dollar or euro. Full internationalisation would require open capital markets, which would mean the party giving up a degree of control it values highly.

## The history that shaped it

The modern story begins with catastrophe. After the communist victory in 1949, Mao Zedong's Great Leap Forward (1958-1962) caused a famine that killed tens of millions, and the Cultural Revolution (1966-1976) shattered institutions and education.

After Mao's death, **Deng Xiaoping** launched "reform and opening" in December 1978. Reform started in the countryside, where the household responsibility system let farmers sell surplus crops, and output surged. Special Economic Zones, beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, invited foreign investors to build export factories. A "dual-track" system let market prices grow up alongside state planning. After the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown reform stalled, until Deng's 1992 "southern tour" revived it.

The 1990s brought tax reform that recentralised revenue in Beijing (a root of today's local government squeeze) and a brutal restructuring of state firms that cost tens of millions of jobs. Joining the World Trade Organization in 2001 then turbocharged exports: China became the world's factory, and the "China shock" reshaped manufacturing employment in America and Europe.

When the global financial crisis hit in 2008, Beijing responded with a stimulus of 4 trillion yuan, plus a far larger surge of bank lending. It kept growth going but began the debt build-up that defines today's problems. A stock market crash and surprise devaluation in 2015 rattled global markets. Xi's era has emphasised control, security and self-reliance; the strict zero-COVID policy, abandoned abruptly at the end of 2022, left scars on confidence that have not fully healed.

## What to watch

- **Consumption.** Does Beijing move decisively to boost household spending, through stronger pensions, healthcare and social insurance, or keep favouring production?
- **Property's floor.** Whether house prices and sales stabilise, and how many developers survive.
- **Prices.** An end to the run of falling factory-gate prices would signal healthier demand.
- **US-China relations.** The durability of the 2025 tariff truces, chip export rules and rare-earth controls.
- **The renminbi.** Any meaningful loosening of capital controls would be a big signal.

## Key takeaways

- China grew rich by saving and investing on a vast scale, becoming the world's dominant manufacturer and largest goods exporter.
- The property sector, once a pillar of growth and household wealth, has been in a deep slump since 2021, exposing heavy local government debt.
- The "new three" industries (EVs, batteries, solar) show real technological strength, but huge output has stoked deflation at home and trade friction abroad.
- A shrinking, ageing population and a cautious private sector make the next phase of growth harder.
- As of mid-2026 Xi Jinping's leadership prioritises control, security and technological self-reliance, and the central question is whether China can shift towards consumption-led growth.
