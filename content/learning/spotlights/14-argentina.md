Economists like to joke that there are four kinds of countries: developed, developing, Japan and Argentina. The punchline is that Argentina did the impossible in reverse. Around 1900 it was among the ten richest countries in the world per person, a land of vast wheat fields and cattle ranches whose capital, Buenos Aires, was built to rival Paris. A century later it had defaulted on its debts nine times, endured hyperinflation and become the International Monetary Fund's largest borrower. Then, in late 2023, voters elected Javier Milei, a self-described "anarcho-capitalist" economist who campaigned brandishing a chainsaw. His shock therapy has cut inflation dramatically. Whether it can deliver lasting growth is one of the most closely watched economic experiments in the world.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 46 million, a third of whom live in Greater Buenos Aires.
- **Economy:** roughly $630 billion in 2024; around $14,000 per person, though estimates swing wildly with the exchange rate.
- **Biggest exports:** soybean meal and oil, maize, wheat, beef, vehicles (mostly to Brazil), crude oil, gold and lithium.
- **Currency:** the peso, which has lost almost all its value against the dollar over the past two decades.
- **System:** a federal presidential republic of 23 provinces plus the capital; presidents serve four-year terms.
- **Track record:** nine sovereign defaults, the most recent in 2020.

## How the economy makes its money

Argentina's most competitive industry is **agriculture**. The Pampas, the fertile grasslands around Buenos Aires, Córdoba and Santa Fe, are among the most productive farmlands on the planet. Argentine farmers pioneered no-till farming and genetically modified seeds, and the country is the world's largest exporter of soybean meal and soybean oil, crushed from beans in huge riverside processing plants near Rosario. It is also a major exporter of maize, wheat, beef, wine and lemons. Farm products and processed food together make up well over half of goods exports.

That makes Argentina vulnerable to the weather. A severe drought in 2023 cut harvests drastically, wiping out an estimated $20 billion of export earnings and helping to tip the economy into recession.

Beyond the farm, Argentina has a sizeable but often uncompetitive **manufacturing** sector, long protected by high tariffs. Car plants around Córdoba and Buenos Aires export mainly to Brazil under the Mercosur trade bloc. A large services sector, including a lively software and tech industry (Mercado Libre, Latin America's e-commerce giant, was founded in Buenos Aires), employs most workers. Informality is high, with over 40% of workers off the books.

### Vaca Muerta and lithium

The newest pillar is energy. **Vaca Muerta** ("Dead Cow"), a shale formation in the Patagonian province of Neuquén, holds some of the largest shale oil and gas resources outside North America. Production has surged as drilling techniques imported from the US shale industry took hold; Argentina's oil output reached record highs in 2025. In 2024 the country ran an energy trade surplus for the first time in over a decade, after years of costly fuel imports. New pipelines and planned LNG export projects could turn energy into a major export earner.

Argentina also sits in the "lithium triangle" with Chile and Bolivia, which holds over half of the world's known lithium resources in salt flats high in the Andes. Lithium projects in the provinces of Jujuy, Salta and Catamarca have expanded fast, making Argentina one of the world's leading producers. Mining copper in the Andes is the next ambition.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Argentina exported goods worth roughly $80 billion in 2024 and ran a sizeable trade surplus, helped by recovering harvests, rising energy exports and weak imports during the recession.

| Export destination | Approx. share of goods exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| Brazil | ~16% |
| China | ~8% |
| United States | ~8% |
| Chile | ~6% |
| India | ~5% |

Brazil, its partner in **Mercosur** (the South American trade bloc founded in 1991 with Uruguay and Paraguay), is the largest buyer of manufactured goods, especially cars, and the biggest supplier of imports, followed by China. China buys soybeans and beef; India buys soybean oil. Mercosur concluded a long-delayed trade agreement with the EU in late 2024, though it still requires ratification. The Milei government has signalled interest in a closer trade deal with the United States.

A peculiarity of Argentine trade is **export taxes** (*retenciones*), especially on soybeans, which have historically been taxed at over 30%. They are a convenient source of hard currency for the government, but farmers resent them and economists argue they discourage production. Milei has cut them in stages.

## Strengths

- **Natural wealth.** World-class farmland, shale oil and gas, lithium and copper.
- **Human capital.** High literacy, free public universities and a well-educated workforce, including a competitive tech sector.
- **Energy turnaround.** Vaca Muerta could make Argentina a significant energy exporter for the first time in decades.
- **Fiscal correction.** Under Milei, the government has run budget surpluses since 2024 for the first time in many years.

## Vulnerabilities

**Credibility.** Generations of defaults, confiscations and frozen savings mean Argentines distrust their own currency. They save in dollars (often kept in cash, "under the mattress") and rush to buy them at the first sign of trouble. Every stabilisation plan must overcome that history.

**Reserves and debt.** The central bank's net foreign-exchange reserves have been very low, and the government faces sizeable dollar debt repayments to bondholders and the IMF. Without access to international markets at reasonable interest rates, rolling over debt is difficult.

**Social strain.** Austerity hit hard at first. Poverty rose to over 50% of the population in the first half of 2024 before falling significantly as inflation slowed. Pensioners, public employees and universities have borne much of the spending cuts.

**Commodity and weather exposure.** Drought or low soybean prices can quickly undermine the dollar inflows the economy depends on.

**Politics.** Milei's party is a minority in Congress and in the provinces, so reforms require deals with others, and policy has historically lurched between market-friendly and interventionist governments.

## Politics and power

For most of the past eight decades Argentine politics has revolved around **Peronism**, the movement founded by Juan Perón in the 1940s, built on trade unions, state intervention and generous social spending. Its most recent incarnation, Kirchnerism (after Néstor Kirchner and Cristina Fernández de Kirchner, presidents between 2003 and 2015), expanded subsidies, nationalised pensions and the oil company YPF, and imposed controls on currency and trade.

**Javier Milei** won the November 2023 run-off with about 56% of the vote, promising to slash the state, end inflation and even replace the peso with the dollar. In office he devalued the official peso by more than half, cut public spending sharply (including transfers to provinces, public works and energy subsidies), halved the number of ministries and laid off tens of thousands of state workers. Monthly inflation fell from about 25% in December 2023 to low single digits within months, and annual inflation dropped from over 200% in 2023 to around 30% by late 2025. A package of reforms, the "Ley Bases", passed in 2024 and included a special regime offering long-term tax and currency guarantees to large investments.

The path has been bumpy. In April 2025 Argentina agreed a new $20 billion programme with the IMF and lifted most of the *cepo*, the web of currency controls, for individuals, letting the peso float within a band. Ahead of the October 2025 mid-term elections, a heavy defeat in Buenos Aires province triggered a run on the peso, and the US Treasury stepped in with an unusual $20 billion currency swap line and direct peso purchases. Milei's party, La Libertad Avanza, then won the mid-terms with around 40% of the vote, strengthening his hand in Congress. As of mid-2026, **Luis Caputo**, a former finance minister and bond trader, runs the economy ministry. The next presidential election is due in 2027.

> Supporters credit Milei with doing what no predecessor dared: balancing the budget and taming inflation. Critics say the cost has fallen on the poorest, and that an overvalued peso and thin reserves make the gains fragile.

## The history that shaped it

Argentina's golden age, from around 1880 to 1930, was built on exporting grain and beef to Britain, financed by British capital and peopled by millions of European immigrants. The Great Depression and the collapse of world trade ended it. A military coup in 1930 began half a century of instability.

Perón, elected in 1946, nationalised railways and utilities, empowered unions and pursued import-substituting industrialisation. He was overthrown in 1955, and for decades power swung between Peronists and military regimes. The 1976-83 dictatorship, which killed or "disappeared" thousands, left behind a heavy foreign debt, and its defeat in the 1982 Falklands War brought democracy back.

The return of democracy coincided with economic chaos. Inflation spiralled into **hyperinflation** in 1989-90, reaching thousands of percent a year. President Carlos Menem's "Convertibility" plan of 1991 pegged the peso one-to-one to the US dollar, backed by reserves, and inflation vanished. But the peg became a straitjacket: when Brazil devalued in 1999 and the dollar strengthened, Argentina could not adjust. In December 2001 the government froze bank deposits (the *corralito*), riots erupted, a string of presidents came and went within weeks, and Argentina defaulted on roughly $100 billion of debt, then the largest sovereign default in history. The peg collapsed in 2002.

A commodity boom fuelled recovery under the Kirchners, but rising spending, money printing, manipulated inflation statistics and capital controls stored up trouble. Mauricio Macri's market-friendly government (2015-19) lifted controls but relied on foreign borrowing; when markets turned in 2018, Argentina received a record IMF loan of $57 billion (of which about $44 billion was disbursed). Alberto Fernández's government (2019-23) restructured debt after a ninth default in 2020, refinanced the IMF loan in 2022 and presided over inflation that exceeded 200% by 2023, setting the stage for Milei.

## What to watch

- **Inflation.** Whether monthly inflation keeps falling towards rates seen in neighbouring countries.
- **Reserves and the peso.** Whether the central bank can rebuild reserves and fully lift remaining controls without a currency crisis.
- **Market access.** A return to borrowing on international markets at reasonable rates would be a milestone.
- **Energy and mining.** New pipelines, LNG projects and copper mines could transform export earnings.
- **Reforms in Congress.** Labour and tax reforms after the 2025 mid-terms.

## Key takeaways

- Argentina is rich in farmland, shale and lithium, yet a century of boom-bust cycles has produced nine sovereign defaults and chronic inflation.
- Agriculture, especially soybeans, dominates exports; Vaca Muerta shale and lithium are new growth engines.
- Javier Milei's shock therapy since December 2023 delivered budget surpluses and cut inflation from over 200% to around 30% by late 2025, at a heavy social cost.
- Thin reserves, a fragile peso and dependence on IMF and US support remain key risks.
- As of mid-2026, strengthened by the 2025 mid-term victory, Milei is pushing deeper reforms ahead of the 2027 presidential election.
