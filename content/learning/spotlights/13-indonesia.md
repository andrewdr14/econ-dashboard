A decade ago, Morowali on the island of Sulawesi was a sleepy coastal district known, if at all, for its forests and fishing. Today it is home to one of the largest nickel-processing complexes on earth, a sprawl of smelters, coal-fired power plants and worker dormitories built largely by Chinese companies. The transformation was not an accident. In 2020 Indonesia banned exports of raw nickel ore, forcing anyone who wanted its vast reserves to process them in the country. It is the boldest example of a strategy Indonesians call *hilirisasi*, or "downstreaming", and it captures the country's big ambition: to stop being a quarry and plantation for richer nations and become an industrial power in its own right. Whether that ambition succeeds will shape the lives of more than 280 million people.

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 280 million, the fourth largest in the world, spread across some 17,000 islands; over half live on Java.
- **Economy:** roughly $1.4 trillion in 2024, the largest in South-East Asia; about $5,000 per person, upper-middle income.
- **Growth:** remarkably steady at around 5% a year for most of the past two decades, outside the pandemic.
- **Biggest exports:** coal, palm oil, iron and steel (mostly nickel-based), nickel products, gas, rubber, garments and footwear.
- **Currency:** the rupiah, which floats and has tended to weaken during bouts of global stress.
- **System:** a presidential democracy since 1998, with directly elected presidents limited to two five-year terms.

## How the economy makes its money

The most important thing to understand about Indonesia's economy is that it runs mainly on its own people. **Household consumption** accounts for around 55% of GDP, and exports for only about a fifth, far less than in trade-dependent neighbours such as Vietnam or Malaysia. A huge domestic market, a growing urban middle class and a young population (median age around 30) keep shops, motorbike dealers, banks and digital platforms busy. This is why Indonesia's growth has been so steady: it is less exposed to swings in global demand than export-led economies.

Beneath that steadiness lies a large informal sector. More than half of workers are informal, many in small family farms, food stalls and micro-businesses. Productivity is low across much of the economy, and the formal manufacturing sector, which once drove growth in labour-intensive goods such as garments and shoes, has been shrinking as a share of GDP since the early 2000s.

The second pillar is **natural resources**. Indonesia is one of the world's great commodity producers:

- **Coal:** it is the world's largest exporter of thermal coal, mostly from Kalimantan (Indonesian Borneo) and Sumatra, sold to China, India and the rest of Asia. Coal also generates most of Indonesia's own electricity.
- **Palm oil:** Indonesia produces more than half of the world's palm oil, the cheap vegetable oil found in everything from instant noodles to shampoo. The industry employs millions, including many smallholders, but expansion has driven deforestation. In 2022 a brief export ban, imposed to curb domestic cooking-oil prices, rattled world markets. The government is also blending palm oil into diesel, moving to a 40% biodiesel blend in 2025.
- **Nickel:** Indonesia holds the world's largest reserves and now produces over half of global mined supply.

### Nickel and downstreaming

Nickel is used in stainless steel and in the cathodes of many electric-vehicle batteries. Indonesia first banned raw ore exports in 2014, relaxed the ban in 2017, and reimposed it fully from January 2020. Investors, overwhelmingly Chinese firms such as Tsingshan, responded by building smelters at industrial parks in Sulawesi and Halmahera. Exports of processed nickel products and stainless steel soared from a few billion dollars to more than $30 billion a year, and Indonesia's share of global nickel output rose dramatically.

Supporters see it as a textbook success of industrial policy: more value added, jobs and tax revenue at home. Critics point to the costs. The smelters run largely on new coal-fired power, giving Indonesian nickel a heavy carbon footprint that worries Western carmakers; there have been deadly accidents and labour disputes; mining has damaged forests and coastal waters; and the flood of supply drove the global nickel price sharply lower, hurting mines elsewhere. The European Union challenged the export ban at the World Trade Organization and won a panel ruling in 2022, but Indonesia appealed into the WTO's non-functioning appeals body, leaving the case in limbo. The government wants to apply the same approach to bauxite, copper and other minerals, with mixed results so far.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Indonesia exported goods worth roughly $265 billion in 2024 and has run consistent trade surpluses in recent years, thanks to commodities. It imports machinery, fuel, chemicals and electronics.

| Export destination | Approx. share of goods exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| China | ~24% |
| United States | ~10% |
| India | ~8% |
| Japan | ~8% |
| ASEAN neighbours | ~18% |

China is overwhelmingly important, both as the largest buyer of coal, nickel products and palm oil and as the largest supplier of imports and a leading investor. Indonesia is a founding member of ASEAN and the RCEP regional trade pact, joined the BRICS group in 2025 and agreed a comprehensive economic partnership with the EU in 2025. In 2025 it negotiated a reduced US tariff rate of 19% after the Trump administration's tariff threats.

## Strengths

- **Size and demographics.** A large, young and urbanising population creates a big domestic market and a potential "demographic dividend" through the 2030s.
- **Resources.** Leading positions in nickel, coal, palm oil, tin, copper and gold give Indonesia bargaining power in the energy transition.
- **Macroeconomic prudence.** A legally capped budget deficit, moderate public debt (around 40% of GDP) and an independent central bank (Bank Indonesia) have earned investment-grade ratings.
- **Resilience.** Steady consumption-driven growth has helped Indonesia ride out global shocks better than many peers.
- **Digital economy.** A vibrant start-up scene, including GoTo (Gojek and Tokopedia), has made Indonesia South-East Asia's largest digital market.

## Vulnerabilities

**Low tax take.** Indonesia collects only around 10% of GDP in taxes, one of the lowest ratios among large economies. That limits spending on infrastructure, education and health, and makes ambitious new programmes hard to fund within the deficit cap.

**The fiscal rule under pressure.** Since 2003, law has capped the budget deficit at 3% of GDP and public debt at 60%. The cap was suspended during the pandemic and restored in 2023. President Prabowo's flagship programmes, notably free nutritious meals for schoolchildren and pregnant women, are very costly, and markets watch closely for any sign the cap might be loosened.

**Commodity dependence.** Coal and palm oil face long-term pressure from climate policies, and commodity price swings move export revenue and the rupiah.

**A squeezed middle class.** Official data suggest the number of Indonesians classified as middle class fell between 2019 and 2024, and good formal jobs remain scarce, especially for the young. Frustration over living costs and perks for politicians spilled into large protests in August 2025.

**Governance.** Corruption, regulatory unpredictability and sudden policy changes (such as the palm oil export ban) deter investors. Critics also worry about the growing role of the military in civilian affairs.

## Politics and power

Indonesia is the world's third-largest democracy, after India and the United States, with a directly elected president, a national parliament and significant power devolved to provinces and districts.

As of mid-2026 the president is **Prabowo Subianto**, a former general and son-in-law of the late dictator Suharto, who won the February 2024 election in the first round with almost 60% of the vote. His running mate, **Gibran Rakabuming Raka**, is the eldest son of the previous president, Joko Widodo ("Jokowi"), who governed from 2014 to 2024 and whose tacit support helped Prabowo win. Prabowo governs with a very broad coalition, leaving little organised opposition in parliament.

Prabowo has promised to lift growth to 8% a year, a goal most economists consider unrealistic. He has created **Danantara**, a sovereign investment fund launched in 2025 that consolidates the government's stakes in big state-owned enterprises, and expanded state-led programmes in food security and nutrition. After the August 2025 protests he replaced Sri Mulyani Indrawati, the long-serving finance minister highly respected by investors, with Purbaya Yudhi Sadewa, who has favoured faster growth. Bank Indonesia has also been encouraged to support government priorities, raising questions among some investors about its independence. The next presidential election is due in 2029.

### Nusantara

In 2019 Jokowi announced that the capital would move from Jakarta, which is congested, polluted and sinking (parts of it by several centimetres a year), to a new city called **Nusantara** in East Kalimantan. The plan, enshrined in law in 2022, carries an estimated cost of more than $30 billion, most of it hoped to come from private investors. Construction of the presidential palace and early government buildings proceeded, but private investment has been slower than hoped, and under Prabowo the project's budget has been scaled back and its timetable stretched. Whether Nusantara becomes a functioning capital or a half-built monument remains open.

## The history that shaped it

Indonesia declared independence from the Netherlands in 1945 after more than three centuries of Dutch colonial rule, winning recognition in 1949 after a bitter war. Its first president, Sukarno, pursued nationalist, left-leaning policies that ended in economic chaos and hyperinflation in the mid-1960s.

In 1965-66, amid an alleged coup attempt, the army under General Suharto seized power; hundreds of thousands of people accused of communist sympathies were killed. Suharto's "New Order" lasted 32 years. Advised by US-trained technocrats, it delivered rapid growth, powered first by oil and then by labour-intensive manufacturing, and lifted many millions out of poverty, but it was deeply corrupt and authoritarian.

The end came with the **Asian financial crisis** of 1997-98. As capital fled the region, the rupiah lost around 80% of its value, banks collapsed and the economy shrank by about 13% in 1998. Riots broke out and Suharto resigned in May 1998. The crisis left deep marks: an enduring caution about foreign borrowing, a strong preference for fiscal rules (the deficit cap dates from this era), more independent and better-regulated finance, and a sweeping political transformation known as *Reformasi*, which brought free elections, press freedom and decentralisation. Direct presidential elections began in 2004, and since then Indonesia has had peaceful transfers of power.

## What to watch

- **The deficit cap.** Whether costly new programmes push the government to loosen the 3% rule.
- **Central bank independence.** How much Bank Indonesia is drawn into financing government priorities.
- **Downstreaming beyond nickel.** Whether processing can be extended to copper, bauxite and battery manufacturing.
- **Nickel and the environment.** Cleaner power for smelters could open Western markets.
- **The rupiah.** It hit record lows in 2025; capital flows remain a key stress indicator.

## Key takeaways

- Indonesia is a large, consumption-driven economy that has grown steadily at about 5% a year for two decades.
- It is a resource giant in nickel, coal and palm oil, and its ban on raw nickel exports has made it the centre of global nickel processing.
- Prudent rules born of the 1997-98 crisis, including a 3% deficit cap, underpin its credibility.
- A low tax take, informality and governance concerns hold back faster growth.
- As of mid-2026 President Prabowo is pursuing ambitious state-led programmes, testing the fiscal discipline that has served Indonesia well.
