In 1989, the land beneath the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was said to be worth more than all the real estate in California. Japanese companies were buying Rockefeller Center and Hollywood studios, and American bestsellers warned that Japan would soon overtake the United States. Then the bubble burst. What followed was not a dramatic collapse but a long, slow stagnation, with falling prices, cautious consumers and a population that began to shrink. For thirty years Japan has been the world's laboratory for problems economists once thought belonged in textbooks. It remains, all the same, one of the richest, safest and most technologically sophisticated societies on earth, and it is now cautiously stepping out of the deflationary era.

## At a glance

- **Population:** roughly 124 million, falling since its peak around 2008.
- **Economy:** the fourth or fifth largest in the world by dollar output, at around $4 trillion in 2024, having been overtaken by Germany in 2023 and by India around 2025.
- **Currency:** the yen, one of the world's main reserve and "safe haven" currencies.
- **Public debt:** gross government debt of roughly 230-250% of GDP, the highest in the rich world.
- **Ageing:** close to 30% of people are aged 65 or over.
- **Signature industries:** cars, industrial machinery, robotics, electronic components and semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

## How the economy makes its money

Japan is often pictured as an export powerhouse, but its economy is driven mainly by domestic demand. Exports of goods and services were worth around a fifth of GDP in 2024, much lower than in Germany or South Korea. Household consumption accounts for more than half of output, and services such as retail, healthcare, property and transport dominate employment.

Manufacturing still contributes about a fifth of output, and it is extraordinarily sophisticated. **Toyota** is the world's largest carmaker by volume, and Honda, Nissan, Suzuki, Subaru and Mazda follow behind. Beyond cars, Japanese firms dominate many unglamorous but essential corners of global supply chains: Fanuc and Yaskawa in industrial robots, Tokyo Electron in chipmaking equipment, Shin-Etsu and Sumco in silicon wafers, Murata in tiny ceramic capacitors found in almost every smartphone, and Keyence in factory sensors. Sony has reinvented itself around games, music, films and image sensors.

A distinctive feature is how much Japanese companies earn abroad. Decades of investment overseas mean that Japan's **primary income**, the profits, dividends and interest flowing home from foreign assets, is now far larger than its trade balance. Japan has been the world's largest net creditor nation for over three decades. In other words, it increasingly lives off the returns on its past savings, rather than just on what it sells today.

Corporate Japan has also changed. After years of hoarding cash and tolerating low returns, companies have been pushed by governance reforms, the Tokyo Stock Exchange and activist investors to raise profitability, unwind cross-shareholdings and return money to shareholders. The Nikkei 225 stock index finally surpassed its 1989 peak in early 2024, some 34 years later.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Japan has few natural resources. It imports almost all its oil and gas, much of its food and most of its metal ores, and pays for them with manufactured goods. When energy prices spike or the yen weakens, the trade balance tends to slip into deficit, as it did in 2022-24.

| Main goods exports (2024, approx.) | Share of goods exports |
|---|---|
| Cars and car parts | ~20% |
| General machinery (incl. chipmaking equipment) | ~19% |
| Electrical machinery and electronic parts | ~18% |
| Chemicals | ~11% |
| Iron and steel | ~4% |

Imports are dominated by energy (liquefied natural gas, crude oil and coal), electronics, pharmaceuticals and food. The **United States** and **China** are each the destination for roughly a fifth or slightly less of Japan's exports, with the US ahead in recent years. China is the largest source of imports, at a little over a fifth. Australia supplies coal, gas and iron ore; the Middle East supplies most of the oil.

Japan has been an energetic advocate of trade agreements, leading the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) after the US withdrew from the original deal in 2017, and signing a broad agreement with the EU. In 2025 it agreed a deal with the Trump administration that set a 15% US tariff on most Japanese goods, including cars, in exchange for large Japanese investment commitments in the US.

## Strengths

- **Social cohesion and stability.** Low crime, low unemployment (typically around 2.5%), and broadly shared living standards.
- **Industrial know-how.** World-leading positions in materials, components and precision equipment that are hard for competitors to replicate.
- **Huge foreign assets.** Net foreign assets of more than $3 trillion provide a steady income stream and a buffer in a crisis.
- **Domestic financing.** Most government debt is held at home, by the central bank, banks, pension funds and insurers, which makes a sudden flight by foreign investors less of a threat.
- **Infrastructure.** Excellent public transport, from the Shinkansen bullet trains to dense urban rail networks.

## Vulnerabilities

**Demography.** Japan's fertility rate is around 1.2 children per woman, far below the 2.1 needed to keep the population stable, and births have fallen to record lows. Fewer workers must support more retirees, and rural areas are emptying: millions of homes stand vacant. Japan has gradually opened up to foreign workers, whose numbers have risen to over two million, but immigration remains politically sensitive.

**Public debt.** Japan's debt is enormous, and for years it was cheap to service because interest rates were near zero. As the Bank of Japan raises rates, interest costs will rise. Because the debt is mostly held domestically and denominated in yen, a Greek-style crisis is unlikely, but the burden constrains future budgets. Long-term government bond yields rose to multi-decade highs in 2025, a sign of how the old arrangements are shifting.

**Productivity.** Japanese output per hour worked lags well behind the US and much of Europe, especially in services. Lifetime employment, seniority-based pay and long working hours can reduce flexibility.

**Energy dependence.** After the Fukushima nuclear disaster in 2011, most reactors were shut down, and fossil fuel imports rose. Restarts have been slow.

**Geopolitics.** Japan sits close to China, North Korea and Russia, and relies heavily on the US security alliance. Tensions with China over Taiwan and disputed islands carry economic risks.

## Politics and power

Japan is a parliamentary democracy with the Emperor as a ceremonial head of state. The **Liberal Democratic Party (LDP)** has governed for almost all of the period since 1955, interrupted only briefly in 1993-94 and 2009-12. Factions within the party have often mattered more than contests with the opposition.

As of mid-2026, the prime minister is **Sanae Takaichi**, who won the LDP leadership in October 2025 and became Japan's first female prime minister. She is a conservative who was close to the late Shinzo Abe and favours expansionary fiscal policy and a stronger defence. Her arrival followed a difficult period for the party: a funding scandal, and losses in elections in 2024 and 2025 that cost the LDP and its long-time partner Komeito their majorities. Komeito left the coalition in October 2025 and the LDP turned to a new arrangement with the Japan Innovation Party (Ishin). Rising prices, especially of rice, have been a potent political issue, as has the role of foreign workers and tourists.

Economic policy is shaped by the interplay between the government, the powerful Ministry of Finance, and the **Bank of Japan (BoJ)**, which has been led since 2023 by Kazuo Ueda, an academic economist.

## The history that shaped it

After defeat in 1945, Japan rebuilt with astonishing speed. Between the mid-1950s and the early 1970s the economy grew by around 10% a year, powered by high savings, close co-operation between government and industry, a cheap yen, and firms that mastered mass production and quality control. The Toyota Production System, with its "just-in-time" inventory and continuous improvement, was copied around the world.

In 1985, the **Plaza Accord** among major economies pushed the yen sharply higher against the dollar. To cushion exporters, the BoJ cut rates, and cheap credit fuelled an enormous bubble in shares and land. When the BoJ tightened in 1989-90, prices collapsed. Banks were left holding vast bad loans that they and regulators were slow to recognise, keeping "zombie" companies alive and choking new lending. The banking crisis peaked in 1997-98.

The result was the **lost decades**. Growth slowed to around 1% a year, and from the late 1990s prices drifted downward. Mild **deflation**, falling prices, might sound pleasant, but it encourages people to delay spending, raises the real burden of debt, and makes it hard for central banks to stimulate demand when interest rates are already at zero. The BoJ pioneered zero interest rates in 1999 and quantitative easing in 2001, tools that other central banks adopted after 2008.

In 2012, Shinzo Abe returned as prime minister promising **Abenomics**: "three arrows" of bold monetary easing, flexible fiscal policy and structural reform. Under Governor Haruhiko Kuroda, the BoJ bought government bonds and even stock funds on a vast scale, introduced a negative interest rate in 2016, and later that year adopted **yield curve control**, pinning the ten-year government bond yield near zero. Abenomics raised employment, especially among women and older people, and ended outright deflation for a time, but it fell short of its 2% inflation target for most of the decade.

Ironically, inflation finally arrived from abroad. After 2022, higher energy prices and a weak yen, which fell to around 160 to the dollar in 2024, pushed prices up. Big companies granted the largest pay rises in three decades in the 2024 and 2025 spring wage negotiations (*shunto*). In March 2024 the BoJ raised rates for the first time in 17 years, ending negative rates and yield curve control, and has continued to raise them gradually since.

## What to watch

- **Wages versus prices.** Whether pay rises keep outpacing inflation, so that households feel better off and spend more.
- **The pace of BoJ tightening.** Each rate rise tests the government's debt sustainability, the banks, and the yen.
- **The yen.** A weak yen helps exporters and tourism but squeezes households paying for imported food and energy.
- **Fiscal policy under Takaichi.** How the government balances stimulus, defence spending and debt.
- **Immigration and automation.** How Japan copes with a shrinking workforce.

## Key takeaways

- Japan's bubble burst around 1990, ushering in decades of slow growth and mild deflation that made it a test case for policies later used across the rich world.
- The economy is driven largely by domestic demand, but Japanese firms remain global leaders in cars, machinery and specialised components.
- Public debt is the highest in the rich world, but it is largely held at home and denominated in yen, which reduces the risk of a sudden crisis.
- Since 2022, inflation and wage growth have returned, and the Bank of Japan has ended negative interest rates and yield curve control.
- A shrinking, ageing population is the defining long-term challenge, and Japan's response is closely watched by other ageing societies.
