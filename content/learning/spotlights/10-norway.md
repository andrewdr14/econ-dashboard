Two days before Christmas in 1969, the American company Phillips Petroleum confirmed what would become the Ekofisk field, deep under the North Sea. Norway, then a modest country of fishermen, shipowners and hydroelectric dams, was about to become very rich. What it did next has become a textbook case. Rather than spending the windfall, Norway salted most of it away in a fund that now owns roughly 1.5% of all the listed shares on the planet, worth well over $300,000 for every Norwegian. It is the most famous answer to the "resource curse", the tendency of oil-rich countries to end up poorer, more corrupt and more unstable than their neighbours. But it raises a new question: what does a country do when it can no longer imagine life without the money?

## At a glance

- **Population:** about 5.6 million.
- **Economy:** roughly $480 billion in 2024; one of the richest countries in the world per person, at around $85,000-90,000.
- **Biggest exports:** natural gas, crude oil, fish (above all farmed salmon), aluminium and other metals, machinery.
- **Currency:** the Norwegian krone, which floats freely and has been notably weak in recent years.
- **System:** a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy; the parliament is the Storting.
- **Europe:** outside the European Union, but inside its single market through the European Economic Area (EEA).

## How the economy makes its money

The oil and gas sector is Norway's economic heavyweight. In a normal year petroleum activities account for something like a fifth of GDP and around three-fifths of goods exports, and that share leapt in 2022 when gas prices exploded. Production is concentrated on the Norwegian continental shelf in the North Sea, the Norwegian Sea and, increasingly, the Barents Sea in the Arctic. The state participates in three ways: through taxes (a special petroleum tax pushes the marginal rate on oil profits to 78%), through direct ownership of a large slice of fields (the State's Direct Financial Interest), and through its roughly two-thirds stake in **Equinor**, the national oil company formerly known as Statoil.

Strip out petroleum and you find what Norwegian statisticians call "mainland Norway", a high-wage, high-productivity economy dominated by services. The public sector is large: health, education and care employ a big share of workers. Hydropower supplies almost all of Norway's electricity, which made power cheap enough to build energy-hungry industries such as aluminium smelting, still a significant export.

### Salmon and shipping

Norway is the world's largest producer of farmed Atlantic salmon, raised in net pens along its long, sheltered fjord coastline. Seafood exports reach well over 150 billion kroner a year in recent years, with salmon about two-thirds of that, sold mainly to Europe and Asia. The industry is profitable but controversial: sea lice, escaped fish and pollution worry environmentalists, and a resource rent tax on aquaculture introduced in 2023 provoked fierce argument with the industry.

Shipping is a centuries-old Norwegian speciality. The country controls one of the world's largest merchant fleets and has a cluster of maritime expertise, from shipbuilding and offshore vessels to classification, insurance and finance, centred around Oslo and Bergen.

### The fund and the fiscal rule

The **Government Pension Fund Global**, usually just called "the oil fund", was set up in 1990 and received its first deposit in 1996. All of the state's net petroleum income flows into it, and it is invested entirely abroad, in shares, bonds, property and renewable energy infrastructure. Its value passed the equivalent of $1.5 trillion in 2023 and has been around $1.8-2 trillion since, making it one of the largest pools of investment capital in the world. It is managed by Norges Bank Investment Management, part of the central bank, and is famously transparent: every holding is published.

The key to the system is the **fiscal rule**, introduced in 2001. The government may spend each year roughly the expected real return on the fund, set at 4% at first and cut to 3% in 2017. That way, in principle, the capital is never spent, and future generations benefit as much as today's. In practice, transfers from the fund now cover around a fifth of the national budget.

> The fund is invested abroad on purpose: spending oil money at home too quickly would overheat the economy and crowd out other industries.

That logic is the defence against **Dutch disease**, named after the Netherlands' experience after it discovered gas in the 1960s. When a resource boom brings in a flood of foreign currency, the local currency rises and wages jump, making other exporters uncompetitive. Has Norway avoided it? Partly. Investing abroad kept much of the money out of the domestic economy. But Norway's labour costs are among the highest in the world, the oil supply industry absorbed many engineers, and outside oil and gas, salmon and aluminium, Norway has relatively few world-scale exporting industries. Some economists argue that the weak krone of recent years is, ironically, giving the non-oil economy a needed boost.

## Trade: what it sells, buys and who with

Norway runs a large trade surplus, driven almost entirely by petroleum. Since Russian pipeline gas to Europe collapsed in 2022, Norway has become Europe's single largest gas supplier, providing around 30% of the gas used in the EU and UK.

| Export | Approx. share of goods exports (2024) |
|---|---|
| Natural gas | ~35% |
| Crude oil | ~25% |
| Fish and seafood | ~10% |
| Metals (mainly aluminium) | ~5% |
| Machinery and other manufactures | ~25% |

Roughly three-quarters of goods exports go to the EU, with Germany and the UK (connected to Norway by gas pipelines) the largest single customers, followed by the Netherlands, Sweden and Poland. Imports, mostly vehicles, machinery, consumer goods and food, come mainly from Sweden, Germany, China and the US.

## Strengths

- **The fund.** A financial buffer larger than the whole economy several times over gives Norway room to cushion recessions and crises, as it did during the pandemic.
- **Trust and institutions.** High trust in government, low corruption and strong collective bargaining underpin the **Nordic model**: generous welfare, free education, and coordinated wage-setting in which export industries set the pace for pay rises.
- **Clean power.** Almost all electricity comes from hydropower, a competitive edge for industry and electrification.
- **Energy security for Europe.** Since 2022 Norwegian gas has given the country new strategic weight.
- **Electric-vehicle leadership.** Thanks to tax breaks, electric cars made up nearly 90% of new car sales in 2024, the highest share in the world.

## Vulnerabilities

**Oil dependence and decline.** Production from older fields will fall over the coming decades, and the global energy transition threatens long-run demand. Petroleum income and its taxes underwrite much of the welfare state.

**Market risk.** The fund's value swings with global stock markets. A large share sits in US equities and technology firms, so a big crash could knock several hundred billion dollars off its value in months.

**Spending creep.** Because the fund has grown faster than expected, 3% of it is a much larger sum than anyone foresaw. Critics warn that the budget has become dependent on fund transfers and that politicians find it ever easier to spend more.

**High costs and productivity.** Mainland productivity growth has slowed, and high wages make it hard to build new exporting industries.

**Electricity politics.** Cables linking Norway to the UK and continental Europe pushed southern Norway's power prices sharply higher in 2022, a political shock in a country used to cheap electricity.

## Politics and power

Norway is a constitutional monarchy with **King Harald V** as head of state, and power lies with the 169-seat Storting, elected every four years under proportional representation. Governments are often minority coalitions that must negotiate budgets with other parties.

As of mid-2026, **Jonas Gahr Støre** of the Labour Party is prime minister. He has led the government since 2021, first in coalition with the agrarian Centre Party, which left in early 2025 in a dispute over adopting EU energy rules. Labour then governed alone and, with former NATO secretary-general **Jens Stoltenberg** brought in as finance minister, won the September 2025 election, continuing as a minority government reliant on support from left-of-centre parties. The main opposition comes from the populist-right Progress Party and the Conservatives.

Big recurring debates include how fast to wind down oil exploration, the wealth tax (which has prompted some wealthy Norwegians to move to Switzerland), the aquaculture tax, and Europe. Norwegians rejected EU membership in referendums in 1972 and 1994. The **EEA** agreement, in force since 1994, gives Norway full access to the single market, but it must adopt most EU rules without a vote on them. Fisheries and agriculture remain outside the deal.

## The history that shaped it

Norway was for centuries a poor periphery of Scandinavia, under Danish and then Swedish rule, until it became fully independent in 1905. Its economy rested on fishing, timber, shipping and, from the early 20th century, hydropower and electro-intensive industry. Nazi occupation in 1940-45 left a strong post-war commitment to national unity and Labour-led social democracy.

The oil discovery of 1969 came at a moment when Norway had time to think. In 1971 parliament adopted the "ten oil commandments", principles insisting that petroleum be managed for the benefit of society, under national control, with a state oil company (Statoil, created in 1972). Foreign companies were welcome but had to transfer know-how.

The road was not smooth. Oil spending in the 1970s and early 1980s fuelled inflation, and a banking crisis in the late 1980s and early 1990s forced the state to take over the largest banks. Those lessons, and the example of other resource-rich nations, persuaded politicians to separate oil income from day-to-day spending, first with the fund and then with the 2001 fiscal rule. The krone has floated since 1992, and Norges Bank has targeted inflation since 2001.

## What to watch

- **The fund's value and the 3% rule.** Whether governments stick to the rule, or the expected return is cut again.
- **Petroleum licensing.** How far the government continues to open new areas, especially in the Arctic.
- **Gas to Europe.** Prices and demand as Europe diversifies and cuts gas use.
- **The krone.** Whether its weakness persists and how Norges Bank responds.
- **New industries.** Offshore wind, carbon capture and hydrogen are the candidates to replace oil, so far with mixed results.

## Key takeaways

- Norway turned an oil discovery into one of the world's largest sovereign wealth funds, worth roughly $1.8-2 trillion in recent years.
- The fiscal rule, spending about 3% of the fund a year, protects both future generations and the domestic economy from overheating.
- Oil and gas still provide most export earnings, and Norway has become Europe's largest gas supplier.
- The Nordic model, salmon, shipping and clean hydropower add depth, but high costs limit new export industries.
- As of mid-2026 Jonas Gahr Støre's Labour government faces the long-term challenge of managing decline in petroleum while resisting the temptation to spend ever more of the fund.
